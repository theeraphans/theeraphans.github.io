Grounded inquiry classification begins with a clear taxonomy

What retrieval can clarify, and what a classifier must still decide across customer messages.

Theeraphan Sukchok · 9 October 2026 · Engineering research note

On this page
Project and evidenceThe workflowDesign choicesAn inquiry exampleOutput contractA testable next stepReferences

Where this question comes from

This note starts from my VOC Inquiry Intelligence work: preparation of emails and calls, a 108-type inquiry taxonomy, retrieval-assisted analysis, human review, and downstream reporting. The public project materials are the evidence for those responsibilities; confidential production code and measurements are not reproduced.

This engineering note develops a testable question from my work. It does not report a new experiment or claim a novel algorithm.

Prepare, classify, then review

The workflow first prepares the message. Emails supply text directly; recorded calls need transcription. Analysis then assigns an inquiry type and extracts information for customer-feedback analysis. The taxonomy covers 108 inquiry types, so classification needs more context than a short list of broad labels.

Retrieval supplies relevant inquiry definitions to the analysis workflow. LlamaIndex and Milvus support that context, while Azure OpenAI supports transcription and classification. LangChain and CrewAI coordinate the workflow, and LangFuse provides traces for investigation.

The important handoff is the review portal. People can inspect the classified inquiry and urgent cases before the reviewed data is delivered to CRM and Power BI. The original message remains the evidence behind the classification.

Do not confuse retrieval with a correct label

A retrieved definition helps an agent interpret a message; it does not prove that the selected label is correct. The system needs both a consistent taxonomy and a way to inspect uncertain or problematic results. Traces help identify where the workflow went wrong, while review keeps operational judgment in the process.

My contribution covered AI analysis, retrieval, and workflow observability. The work connects message-level processing to the data that operations teams use, rather than stopping at a generated summary.

One message can contain several business questions

Consider an illustrative email: “My shipment is late, and I need to change the delivery address.” It contains a shipment concern and a requested action. The example is fabricated for explanation; it is neither a customer record nor an assertion about the production label policy.

The taxonomy needs to say what an inquiry type represents. A topic, a requested action, and a responsible department are different dimensions. Combining those meanings can cause disagreement even when the model and reviewer both understand the message.

Retrieval supplies relevant definitions, while classification selects an output that fits the business taxonomy. Preserving the retrieved context for investigation helps distinguish a wrong definition from a wrong interpretation.

Calls add another failure point. A misheard product identifier or negation can change the prepared text before classification begins. A reviewer needs access to the evidence behind the label, subject to the organization's data-access policy.

Make the downstream record predictable

A summary is helpful to readers, while reporting needs consistent fields and known meanings for missing values. Otherwise identical issues can fragment into different dashboard categories because the generated wording changes.

This table describes candidate record responsibilities, not the internal production schema.

A taxonomy change can alter reporting even when customer behavior stays constant. Merging two labels should therefore be visible in both the evaluation set and the interpretation of historical trends.

What I would test next

Compare taxonomy-only classification with retrieval-assisted classification on the same reviewed messages. Preserve the retrieved definitions beside each prediction. Include similar categories and multiple intents, and keep the output policy fixed. Inspect whether retrieval supplied useful definitions or distracting context.

This is a proposed test, not a completed result. A future report needs its fixtures, baseline, and execution evidence.
