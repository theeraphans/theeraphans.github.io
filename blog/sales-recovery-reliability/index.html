Reliable follow-up and honest measurement of sales recovery

Inspecting retries and duplicate actions without confusing activity with commercial impact.

Theeraphan Sukchok · 9 October 2026 · Engineering research note

Where this question comes from

This note starts from my Sales Recovery workflow, which connects quotation and order information, business rules, scheduled monitoring, and Power Automate follow-up. Internal matching rules and commercial outcomes are not disclosed. The examples below explain design risks rather than report internal incidents.

This engineering note develops a testable question from my work. It does not report a new experiment or claim a novel algorithm.

Keep the decision tied to the sales process

Timing matters: a quotation can be open for a legitimate business reason. Lead-time context and follow-up rules help avoid reducing the problem to “old quotation equals urgent.” A useful automation should preserve that context when it hands work to a person.

My work focused on the decision workflow and its integration with business automation. This public description does not specify a customer-send approval policy or expose internal customer records.

A retry should not create another follow-up

A scheduled job can fail after some actions have succeeded. Its retry needs to distinguish an opportunity still needing work from an action already accepted by the destination.

Airflow recommends repeatable task behavior and a consistent data interval across reruns. That is a reliability requirement to examine here, rather than evidence of the workflow's exact replay implementation. Airflow best practices.

An illustrative action identity could combine an opportunity, action type, and follow-up window. Its accepted status would belong in an action record, separate from the scheduler's task status. The exact identity depends on the business policy.

The difficult test is an accepted action followed by a timeout at the caller. Retrying blindly can duplicate work. A fake destination that accepts the action and delays its response provides a safe way to test that boundary.

Activity and commercial impact need different measures

Tasks created measure activity. They do not establish additional sales caused by the workflow. A customer may have ordered anyway, and the sales team may have intervened for reasons the workflow did not observe.

A proposed evaluation begins with selection quality, duplicate-action rate, and whether the team can act on the output. Commercial impact needs a separate comparison with a defined population and observation window. A controlled rollout can support a stronger claim than totaling every quotation that was followed by an order.

Follow-up also has a capacity constraint. Increasing the number of selected opportunities can overwhelm the team and make the highest-value ones harder to find. Ranking quality should be inspected at the volume the business can actually handle.

No comparison experiment or conversion uplift is published here. My public evidence supports the workflow and its purpose; exact revenue figures remain confidential.

What I would test next

Simulate a follow-up endpoint accepting a task but delaying its response until timeout. Replay the decision and inspect duplicate effects. Separately define a commercial observation window and comparison population. Increased notifications demonstrate activity; incremental revenue requires a controlled outcome comparison.

This is a proposed test, not a completed result. A future report needs its fixtures, baseline, and execution evidence.
