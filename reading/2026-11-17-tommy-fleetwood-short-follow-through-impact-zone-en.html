The Finish Is Evidence, Not the Move

Tommy Fleetwood's short, punchy follow-through works because the club has already traveled through a controlled impact zone. The finish reveals a centered turn and a path that supports his draw without sending the club too far to the right.

4:49 videoGolf Digest · Luke Kerr-DineenPublished January 25, 2024Summary run: November 17, 2026 · 06:50 ICT

Executive summary

Fleetwood shortened the exit to keep his draw under control

Tommy Fleetwood grew up shaping the ball from right to left. That pattern still defines his stock shot. The club approaches impact slightly from inside the target line, while the face points a little left of the club's path. Golf Digest describes the gap as roughly two degrees. The ball can begin right of the target and curve back without becoming a hook.

The danger sits on the same side of the pattern. When Fleetwood's path travels too far to the right, the draw can start too far right, climb, and turn sharply left. His older swings show two versions of that problem. As a child, his arms became trapped behind him and then flung toward right field with a high exit and active wrist roll. Later, he added so much side bend that his head moved down and back while his upper body stayed behind his lower body.

His current abbreviated follow-through is the visible result of a different organization. He keeps the upper body more centered while turning, clears the lead hip, and lets the body carry the club around him. The path still supports a draw, but it does not keep traveling right for as long. Fleetwood built this pattern by rehearsing the impact zone and hitting thousands of punch shots until the shorter exit became part of his full swing.

Read the evidence

The follow-through tells you what the club was doing earlier

The finish happens after the ball has left, so it cannot rescue a poor strike. It can still reveal how the player arrived at impact. Fleetwood's left arm stays relatively close to his torso and the club exits around him. Those clues fit a body that continues turning rather than arms that keep extending away from the target line.

This distinction matters for practice. A golfer can stop the arms deliberately and create a short-looking finish while leaving the underlying delivery unchanged. The photograph may resemble Fleetwood for a fraction of a second, but the strike, start line, and curve will expose the difference.

Ball-flight geometry

A small face-to-path gap produces the draw he wants

For a right-handed player, a draw usually requires the clubface to point left of the club's path at impact. Both can still point right of the final target. The face influences the ball's initial direction, while the face-to-path relationship helps determine curvature.

The useful lesson is not to chase a universal two-degree number. It is to connect the player's path and face pattern with the ball's start line and curve. Fleetwood's finish makes sense because it contains the path tendency he already owns.

The old pattern

His arms once escaped high and right

Childhood footage shows a strong version of Fleetwood's natural draw. His arms move behind him, then release toward the right with visible wrist rotation. The club finishes high and away from the body. Golf Digest uses this footage to show that his current motion did not appear by accident. It is the product of years spent moderating an early tendency.

The modern swing keeps the draw but changes its size. That is a better model for improvement than replacing a player's identity. Fleetwood did not need to turn himself into a fade player. He needed a narrower version of his preferred geometry.

A second compensation

Excessive side bend left his upper body too far behind

Footage from 2009 shows Fleetwood's head moving down and back through the strike. His upper body tilts away while the lower body continues opening. Some side bend belongs in a powerful golf swing, but the source argues that Fleetwood's amount became excessive. It created space for the club to attack too far from the inside.

This is where copying body positions becomes risky. A golfer who already cuts across the ball may need a different change. Reducing side bend because Fleetwood once had too much can move that golfer farther from a functional delivery.

The current solution

Stay centered, clear the lead hip, and turn the club around

Fleetwood now keeps his upper body more on top of the strike while the lead hip clears. His demonstration is simple. The lower body opens, the torso continues rotating, and the arms travel with that turn. The club exits around the body instead of continuing toward right field.

The phrase "stay centered" should not become "freeze the head." Fleetwood still shifts, tilts, and rotates. Centered describes the overall relationship between his upper body and the strike, especially compared with the older move that dropped his head severely down and back.

How he trained it

Own the club-parallel window before worrying about the finish

Fleetwood says he worked on the area from the club reaching roughly parallel to the ground before impact until it reaches parallel after impact. Coaches often call these positions P6 and P8. The labels matter less than the size of the practice window. It is short enough to rehearse slowly and close enough to impact that the player can connect the motion with contact and ball flight.

He then hit many punch shots. The ball gave immediate feedback. A path that stayed too far right produced the wrong start line or too much curve. Repetition gradually allowed the abbreviated exit to remain when speed and swing length increased.

Alignment-stick drill

Build a corridor that exposes a path moving too far right

The video shows alignment sticks on the ground around the ball and target line. Their job is visual. They make it easier to see when the club's travel and the ball's launch are drifting too far to the right. Fleetwood can preserve a small in-to-out relationship without letting that relationship grow unchecked.

Safe setup. Never point an alignment stick toward the body or place it where the club can strike and launch it. Use flat ground references, begin with half swings, and move the sticks farther away if they attract attention during the motion.

A practical session

Copy the feedback loop, not Fleetwood's exact pose

  1. Map ten stock shots. Record strike location, start line, curve, and finish balance. Do not change anything yet.
  2. Find the impact window. Make waist-high rehearsals from club parallel before impact to club parallel after it. Let the torso keep turning.
  3. Hit ten punch shots. Use half speed and a short finish. Judge the ball, especially its start line and curve.
  4. Add ground references. Lay two alignment sticks safely outside the strike area. Use them to see the target line and intended launch corridor.
  5. Lengthen only when the flight holds. Move to three-quarter swings, then full swings. Keep the shorter version if longer swings restore the old miss.
  6. Finish with five random targets. Change club or target between shots. A pattern that survives a new task is more useful than one that only works in a block.

What to copy

The training logic transfers better than the body positions

  • Copy the diagnosis. Use the finish to ask what happened through impact.
  • Copy the progression. Rehearsal, punch shot, three-quarter swing, then full speed.
  • Copy the external references. Start line, curve, strike, and safe alignment sticks provide evidence.
  • Do not copy the number blindly. A two-degree face-to-path gap only works inside the player's full path, face, speed, and target pattern.
  • Do not force the arms to stop. Let the compact finish emerge from a turn that carries the club around the body.

My take

The short finish is a constraint that protects Fleetwood's preferred shot

Fleetwood's motion is interesting because it preserves rather than erases his draw. He did not rebuild toward a neutral-looking swing for aesthetic reasons. He reduced the part of his old pattern that made the left miss too large.

The best lesson is causal order. Ball flight identifies the problem. The impact-zone rehearsal changes the delivery. The finish becomes evidence that the new relationship held together. Starting from the finish and posing there reverses that order.

For a golfer trying to learn from Fleetwood, the right question is not "How do I stop this short?" Ask whether the club path, face, strike, and body turn need a smaller version of the same pattern. The answer should come from the ball.
