How Tommy Fleetwood Makes Fairway Woods Predictable

Fleetwood treats the lie as an instruction. A flat fairway asks for an iron-like swing, a downhill lie supplies the curve, wind changes launch, and rough can turn a 9-wood into a long-range recovery club.

15:10 videoTaylorMade Golf Canada · Tommy FleetwoodPublished October 10, 2025Summary run: November 13, 2026 · 11:57 ICT

Executive summary

The lie decides the shot before the swing begins

This lesson follows Fleetwood and Johnson Wagner through three environments: a clean fairway, a downhill ball-below-the-feet lie, and rough. The useful pattern is Fleetwood's refusal to force one fairway-wood technique onto all three.

From a flat lie, he wants his 5-wood to continue the same motion he uses through the iron set. His attack angle approaches level, but he does not hang back and scoop the ball into the air. On the slope, he accepts the fade the ground is likely to produce, aims left, and moves pressure toward his heels to counter the pull toward his toes. From rough, the 9-wood becomes a different tool. He uses a steeper, shorter action that feels like a punch 7-iron and lets the club's loft and head mass lift the ball.

The club choice follows the same logic. A 5-wood supplies a stock carry around 250 to 260 yards. A 9-wood flies roughly 10 to 15 yards beyond his 4-iron, lands more softly, and can escape lies where a long iron would force a layup. Fleetwood chooses between them by asking which trajectory the course needs, rather than filling a textbook loft gap.

Top-of-bag design

A 9-wood earns its place when the course asks for height

Fleetwood describes several options above his irons. A mini driver is the longest. A 5-wood is a regular part of the setup, and a 9-wood often enters the bag when the course creates the right problem.

The obvious reason is recurring yardage, but firmness matters just as much. Players often see a hard, fast course and reach for a 2-iron because the low flight appears controllable. Fleetwood points out the opposite requirement. When the ground is already adding speed, a high 9-wood or 5-wood can stop the ball sooner. The club controls what happens after landing.

Wind is the counterargument. A 9-wood may offer fewer flight options when the course is exposed and the ball must stay low. Fleetwood therefore treats it as a course-specific club, not a permanent answer for every setup.

Flat fairway

Choose the target from carry and miss before discussing mechanics

The first full shot is 248 yards to a right-side flag. Fleetwood's 5-wood normally carries between 250 and 260 yards, so the number fits a stock shot. His target still depends on the green. Firmness, wind, bunkers, long trouble, and the space beside the green determine whether the flag is worth attacking.

Shot shape changes the decision. If the target were closer, Fleetwood could start a fade from the left and let it work toward the pin. At 260 yards, that shape may lose too much distance to reach. Aiming at the middle is not automatically conservative either. A player 250 yards away can miss the middle target. The plan needs a preferred miss, not a comforting label.

Delivery from a clean lie

Let the fairway wood continue the iron set

Fleetwood wants the 5-wood and 9-wood motion to feel close to his irons. He moves through 7-iron, 6-iron, 5-iron, and 4-iron without inventing a new swing when the head changes from iron to wood. Constancy reduces the temptation to help the ball upward.

His fairway-wood attack angle is where the delivery approaches zero. His irons remain downward and his driver moves upward. Level does not mean a scoop. When Fleetwood wants to train more coverage, he sometimes practises taking a small divot with the wood. The exercise keeps the chest moving through the ball and prevents the body from falling behind it.

This distinction matters because a modern fairway wood already has loft and a low center of gravity. If the player adds side bend and hangs back to create height, Fleetwood says his own swing can develop a two-way miss. The club should launch the ball. The player still has to deliver the face and low point.

Downhill and ball below feet

Do not fight the curve the ground is already producing

On the second location, the ball sits below the feet on a downslope. Fleetwood expects the lie to move the ball left to right. His rule is to accept the shot the ground gives him. He starts farther left and lets the slope bring the ball toward the target.

The balance adjustment solves a separate problem. A downhill, ball-below lie pulls the body toward the toes. Fleetwood begins with more pressure in his heels to counter that force. On a steeper downslope, he would also tilt his pressure and body to match the ground.

Wagner worries about a double cross because his stock shape is a draw. Fleetwood's answer is course-management as much as technique. Give the fade enough room. Aiming too close to the final target can make the player resist the expected curve. Starting far enough left makes the slope-driven shot easier to accept.

The useful miss. Fleetwood starts one attempt too far left, but the ball still curves in the expected direction and finishes in a playable area. He calls it the proper miss. The shot pattern matched the plan even though the start line was imperfect.

Flighting the ball

Lower the launch without abandoning the original shape

When the wind strengthens, Fleetwood keeps the same leftward allowance and expected fade. He moves the ball slightly back and tries to compress it. The adjustment changes launch while the slope continues to control the broad shape.

Wagner's following shot shows the visible evidence. He produces a lower flight and takes a divot. Fleetwood reads that divot as proof that Wagner covered the ball and delivered the club through it instead of trying to lift it.

9-wood from rough

Use a short-iron intention with a high-lofted wood

Fleetwood's 9-wood carries about 235 yards, roughly 10 to 15 yards beyond a well-struck 4-iron. The number alone does not explain the club. The 9-wood launches higher and lands softer. Fleetwood compares the flight to a pitching wedge travelling 235 yards.

Rough creates the stronger case. A drive can miss the fairway by two yards and leave a lie where a 3-iron or 4-iron cannot reach the green. The 9-wood has more loft, a larger head, and enough mass to move through the grass. It can turn an automatic layup into a chance to advance the ball near or onto the green.

The motion comes from a lesson Fleetwood attributes to Tiger Woods. Imagine a punch 7-iron through trees. Open the stance a little and use a short, steep action. The player hits down rather than trying to sweep the ball out. Loft and head design supply the launch.

The exact lie still decides the target. Fleetwood sees one ball sitting down but expects it to come out fast enough to reach a back flag around 250 yards. His shot launches cleanly and finishes on the green. The hosts react as if it may have gone in, but the video does not establish the final resting place. The reliable evidence is the launch, distance, and green reach from rough.

A practical session

Test four fairway-wood jobs instead of chasing one perfect swing

  1. Map the stock carry. Hit eight balls from a clean lie. Record carry, strike, and curve. Use the middle group, not the longest shot, as the stock range.
  2. Find the low point. Alternate normal swings with swings that produce a small brush or divot after the ball. Stop if the motion becomes steep and heavy.
  3. Rehearse a slope-driven curve. Use a modest ball-below lie. Aim far enough left to accept the fade and compare pressure in the heels with your normal setup.
  4. Build a flighted option. Move the ball slightly back and reduce launch into a headwind. Check whether carry loss and dispersion still make the shot useful.
  5. Test rough honestly. Try several lies with a 7-wood or 9-wood. Record which depths still allow clean launch and which require a layup.

My take

The secret is permission to hit the shot the lie prefers

The title promises a fairway-wood secret, but the lesson is mostly about removing unnecessary resistance. Fleetwood does not try to turn a downhill ball-below lie into a straight stock shot. He does not ask a long iron to escape rough because its yardage looks correct on paper. He lets the ground, grass, wind, and landing surface narrow the choices.

This makes the technique easier to understand. Pressure in the heels has a job. It counters the slope. The punch 7-iron feeling has a job. It moves the 9-wood through rough. A near-level 5-wood delivery has a job. It preserves the iron-like motion without adding a scoop. Every cue belongs to a visible problem, so the player can judge it by the resulting strike and flight.
