Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback

The problem

What was missing before this work

Pretraining rewards next-token prediction over internet text, not helpful responses to user intent. Larger base models can be capable while ignoring instructions, producing unwanted continuations, or exploiting ambiguous prompts.

The paper seeks a practical behavioral-training pipeline using human judgments without requiring annotators to write an ideal answer for every possible prompt.

Core idea

The paper in one idea

Collect human-written demonstrations and fine-tune a base GPT-3 model. Then sample multiple answers, ask labelers to rank them, train a reward model to predict those rankings, and optimize the policy against the learned reward while constraining drift from supervised behavior.

The three stages separate imitation from preference learning and policy optimization. Human feedback defines the target distribution; the reward model scales that feedback to more generated samples.

maximize E[reward_model(x,y) − β KL(π(y|x) || π_SFT(y|x))]

Mechanism

How the method works

Prompts come from API usage and labeler-written examples. Supervised fine-tuning teaches a starting policy. Pairwise rankings train a scalar reward model. PPO updates the policy, with a KL penalty and a pretraining-data term used to limit capability regression.

Evaluation returns to humans: labelers compare outputs for helpfulness, truthfulness, and harmlessness, while automated benchmarks check whether alignment erased core abilities.

Technical deep dive

The machinery that matters

The reward model uses a Bradley-Terry-style pairwise objective: preferred completions should receive higher scalar scores. PPO treats complete responses as trajectories and the reward-model output as terminal reward.

KL regularization is essential because optimizing an imperfect learned reward can move the policy into regions where the reward model is unreliable. This is an early form of controlling reward hacking.

Evidence

What the experiments established

Labelers preferred outputs from a 1.3B InstructGPT model over base GPT-3 175B outputs in the reported comparisons, showing behavioral training can outweigh raw scale for instruction following. The models also improved on truthfulness and toxicity evaluations without broad benchmark collapse.

The evaluators were aligned with the project’s labeling guidance and largely English-speaking context. Preference rates are conditional on that population, prompt sample, and policy.

Why it mattered

How it changed the field

InstructGPT established the modern assistant post-training pipeline and made alignment data, reward modeling, and online evaluation core product infrastructure.

It also exposed a new failure surface: models can optimize the evaluator’s proxy, so reward-model drift, annotator consistency, and production feedback require continuous measurement.

Engineering use

What an AI engineer should do with it

Version prompt sources, labeling instructions, annotator groups, reward models, and policy checkpoints as one lineage. Hold out adversarial prompts and compare against both the base and SFT models.

Use gateway-level safety controls in addition to model behavior. RLHF can reduce unwanted output frequency but does not guarantee authorization, privacy, or correct tool execution.

Limits and later corrections

Where the result stops

A reward model inherits annotator biases and becomes less reliable off-distribution. PPO is operationally complex and sensitive to reward scale, KL strength, sampling, and policy instability.

Helpful, honest, and harmless can conflict. The paper improves measured preferences; it does not solve value pluralism, factual verification, or specification gaming.

Takeaways

What to remember

  • Pretraining capability and instruction-following behavior are different objectives.
  • SFT supplies a stable starting policy.
  • Human rankings can train a scalable reward proxy.
  • KL regularization limits destructive policy drift.
  • Small aligned models can be preferred over much larger base models.
  • Preference optimization is not a guarantee of truth or safety.
