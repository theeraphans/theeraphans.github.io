Executive summary

Useful AI changes a capability or a constraint

The readable sessions share a consistent message: generating a slide, picture, or answer is only an entry point. Business value appears when AI improves a real capability, removes an operational constraint, preserves domain knowledge, or makes a previously difficult service accessible.

The Google Cloud session emphasizes judgment and human relationships. Sea explains leadership-driven adoption and applications in gaming and commerce. AWS presents Thai enterprise cases and staged software development. The SME session shows why a faster non-bottleneck does not improve total throughput. The creator session explains why a clear creative brief matters more than chasing tools alone.

Understand the work

Define the customer problem, domain facts, and actual constraint.

Build the capability

Connect AI to relevant knowledge, workflows, tools, and human decisions.

Measure the result

Look at throughput, quality, cost, customer outcomes, and risk.

Session 1 · Approximately 00:45:39–01:11:14

Google Cloud: unlock human capability, not only speed

The speaker introduced as Sarut Tipsena, Google Cloud Thailand's country manager, frames AI as a technological shift rather than a single tool. His electricity analogy encourages a broader question: not just how to do today's task faster, but what becomes possible when a new general capability is available.

Scientific examples expand the frame

He discusses genomics, protein research, and weather prediction to show that AI's effects extend beyond office content. The genome example describes models helping researchers form hypotheses and investigate how DNA variants affect biological processes. The protein example illustrates faster scientific investigation, while the weather example emphasizes that better predictions can change disaster planning.

These are speaker-presented examples, not evidence that AI has solved medicine or made forecasting infallible. The caption renders some product names and measurements inconsistently, so this article does not assert an exact model version, grid resolution, or research speedup as verified.

A scientific wording correction

The talk's “2% versus 98%” discussion should not be read as a literal measure of all human understanding of DNA. About 2% of the genome codes for proteins; the remainder is non-coding and includes important regulatory regions. Google DeepMind's AlphaGenome explanation supports that distinction.

Five adaptation ideas

  1. Use AI as a multiplier. Apply it to expertise rather than assuming fluent output replaces expertise.
  2. Think beyond productivity. Ask which capability can become accessible, not only which task can become faster.
  3. Improve questions and judgment. A wrong question answered quickly can amplify a wrong direction. Decide what matters and inspect information critically.
  4. Use AI to access knowledge. Difficult subjects can become easier to approach through explanation, while important claims still need verification.
  5. Preserve human value. Listening, empathy, coordination, and communication remain central when people work with people.

The presentation example makes the last point concrete: producing slides becomes easier, but helping an audience understand and remember an argument still depends on how it is communicated. The broader claim that AI can never perform a particular human activity is best read as emphasis, not a permanent technical prediction.

Session 2 · Approximately 01:13:11–01:39:00

Sea: adoption needs leadership, practice, and domain fit

The speaker introduced as Surut Wanichphan, a senior director at Sea Thailand, describes adaptation across gaming, commerce, and financial services. His argument is that an incumbent technology company is not protected from the next transition simply because it succeeded in the previous one.

Adoption is organizational work

He describes a leadership mandate, training, an AI community hub, and expectations that people build and try agents themselves. Senior leaders review progress, and the expectation spreads through teams. The intended mechanism combines top-down commitment with bottom-up experimentation.

Tools alone are insufficient. Sea also pursues regional language capability and internal system integration. The speaker says the company uses external tools while developing its own language-model capability for Southeast Asian languages. The summary does not infer an undisclosed architecture or model name.

Gaming: behavior and production pipelines

Examples include a football goalkeeper trained against player patterns and racing opponents that learn stronger driving behavior. These illustrate AI improving the experience itself, rather than merely generating promotional copy. The gaming examples include partner technologies; they should not all be attributed to Sea as original research.

He also reports faster character creation, contrasting a previous multi-month pipeline with a shorter AI-assisted process. This is a presentation anecdote, not a controlled estimate of every studio's productivity.

Commerce: reduce uncertainty and seller workload

The Shopee “AI Look” example addresses a limitation of online fashion shopping: customers cannot physically try the item. A photo-based visualization can help them imagine its appearance. It does not establish accurate physical fit or guarantee that the delivered garment will match a generated image.

A market-analysis example discusses collecting and examining product listings, such as sunscreen categories and top sellers. Its useful idea is quicker market investigation, not a promise that a chatbot has unrestricted, complete, or always-current access to marketplace data.

Cross-border selling uses translation and localized storefront support to reduce language barriers. Sellers can communicate in Thai while serving other markets. Operational obligations such as fulfillment, local rules, and accurate listings do not disappear with translation.

Hackathons turn intent into artifacts

The speaker describes a regional hackathon evaluated on working prototypes, not ideas alone. Examples include adaptive game difficulty, turning travel clips into itineraries, and assisting livestream sellers with repetitive chat questions. These are prototypes demonstrating possibilities, not proof of production reliability.

Session 4 · Approximately 02:07:19–02:35:07

AWS: connect AI to the organization's actual workflow

The speaker introduced as Vasan Thirapatpong, AWS Thailand's country manager, distinguishes individual AI use from a capability that scales within an organization. Summarizing meetings or creating images can be useful, but those activities alone do not establish a business platform.

He quotes a high rate of unsuccessful enterprise AI adoption. The original study and its definition are not verified here, so this article does not repeat the percentage as a settled fact. His substantive point is that many experiments fail to extend into organizational context.

1. Big C: shopping by intent

Instead of requiring a customer to name each product, a conversational service can start with a goal such as making a minced-pork omelet, identify ingredients, and present purchasable items. The speaker describes Big C's earlier chat-commerce model and its dependence on human agents, then presents an AI-assisted “Biggy” beta.

The backend matters: product availability, branch information, model routing, off-topic controls, and token limits connect a conversation to an actual commercial service. The example illustrates moving from an answer to a grounded shopping workflow.

2. 7-Eleven: branch-specific forecasting

Stores near schools, hospitals, or factories do not face identical demand. School holidays, weather, and local activities also change demand over time. Forecasting therefore needs local and temporal context rather than distributing the same inventory everywhere.

The speaker reports reducing stockouts while also lowering inventory. The important mechanism is better allocation, not simply holding more stock. The reported gains remain speaker claims; this summary does not verify the methodology or interpret them as a universal result.

3. Thai Oil: preserve expertise and operational context

The knowledge example combines engineering drawings, inspection records, standards, email, and interviews with experienced employees approaching retirement. A generic question-answer system was not enough; useful answers needed accumulated institutional context.

Procurement advice provides a concrete distinction. Listing purchase-request and purchase-order steps is shallow. Useful organizational guidance includes restrictions against splitting orders and checks that suppliers are approved. The example threshold is contextual to the presentation, not a rule for other companies.

The speaker reports large time savings and a financial value estimate from a pilot involving roughly 50 people, then projects expansion to a much larger workforce. A projected scale-up is not the same as realized savings. Nor does saved time automatically become cash profit.

Knowledge assistance is not operational authorization

The refinery discussion explicitly includes sensitive maintenance and change processes. An answer about equipment is not permission to perform hazardous work. Engineering review, permits, and authoritative procedures remain separate controls.

4. OR: location analysis and customer experience

A new station proposal can be evaluated using location, surrounding competitors, traffic, and possible cannibalization of existing stations. This moves beyond checking whether an application meets a simple checklist.

A second example uses customer identity to personalize offers when a vehicle arrives. The speaker explicitly mentions obtaining consent. This summary does not treat that brief mention as a complete assessment of privacy compliance.

5. Sansiri: invoice work beyond OCR

Extracting characters is only the first step. The system also checks contextual inconsistencies between invoice information and payment-related records. The speaker describes a high-volume workflow and substantially reduced processing effort.

The distinction is extraction versus validation: readable text is not automatically correct financial data, and an inconsistency flag still needs a defined handling process.

Software development: stage the human decisions

The talk contrasts a one-shot approach, where AI runs until a final result appears, with a staged AI-driven development process. Requirements become specifications, humans review them, design is inspected, and implementation follows. The speaker references AI-DLC and spec-driven tools; caption spellings vary.

Requirement and specification

Make the intended behavior explicit and correct misunderstandings.

Design review

Inspect structure before allowing implementation to spread.

Implementation and validation

Generate code with human checkpoints, not one initial prompt alone.

The closing enterprise-assistant example discusses connecting work information and building a knowledge graph. Product capabilities are presented by the speaker; this article does not infer that every interaction is retained safely or that more memory guarantees more accurate answers.

Session 5 · Approximately 02:37:16–03:07:15

SMEs: fix the bottleneck before multiplying activity

The speaker introduced as Pongsathorn Thanabodeepat, who calls himself Nop, argues that strong selling does not compensate for weak management and financial operations. A founder may hire people while remaining the approval point for everything, becoming the company's constraint.

His distinction is working inside the business versus designing the business as a system. Technical or professional skill in the product is not automatically management skill. The national comparisons and historical business examples in the talk are not independently verified statistics here.

The coffee-shop exercise

Three stations take 30, 45, and 60 seconds per cup. With overlapping work across stations, the slowest station limits steady-state throughput to 60 cups per hour. Making the cashier twice as fast does not improve that maximum because the 60-second station still constrains the line.

Moving the milk refrigerator closer reduces the final station to 40 seconds. The constraint then moves to the 45-second espresso station, giving 3,600 ÷ 45 = 80 cups per hour, a one-third increase. It is not 90 cups per hour: calculating only 3,600 ÷ 40 would overlook the new bottleneck.

The AI connection

Accelerating the wrong stage may generate more unfinished work rather than more business output. Identify the constraint, improve it, then measure where the constraint moved.

Return on time investment

Nop emphasizes investing time now to prevent repetitive work later. The example of carrying water versus building a delivery system explains why process improvement can feel slower initially while creating recurring capacity.

Another example describes thousands of orders overwhelming manual tax-invoice processing. The commercial success exposed an unprepared back office. A simpler self-service collection and processing system can remove that burden without requiring an elaborate AI agent.

Map the process before generating software

A B2B onboarding illustration crosses sales, legal, accounting, and IT: receive an application, inspect a contract, evaluate payment terms, enter records, and provision access. The speaker maps the work as swimlanes and demonstrates an AI-generated system based on the process description.

He reports a short generation time and low token cost. Those are prototype-generation claims, not the total cost of production delivery. Security, integrations, testing, access management, hosting, and maintenance still need work. The process model makes the prototype useful; cheap code generation does not prove the system is ready for real customers.

Session 6 · Approximately 03:14–03:34:00

Creators: the brief comes before the tool

The speaker identified at the close as Khajorn Jiaranaipanich explains four output modes: writing, images, audio, and video. His central argument is that creators often ask which tool produced an output while overlooking the direction supplied to that tool.

He offers seven dimensions of a creative brief: what to create, who it is for, where it appears or is set, style, composition, color and lighting, and what should be excluded.

Specificity expresses intent

“Beautiful coffee” does not specify the drink, purpose, audience, or visual treatment. A brief for a slide differs from one for a social advertisement. The audience for a particular post may also differ from the business's overall customer base.

Location includes the scene itself, not just the province or city. A product on a table, in a bedroom, or at an airport implies different visual context. Style describes a presentation direction; composition decides where the product sits and whether space remains for other elements.

Color and light establish mood and brand consistency. Exclusions constrain common unwanted additions: crowds, text, clutter, or excessive blur. These are practical recommendations, not universal rules that every model follows deterministically.

A video-editing demonstration

The speaker shows AI assembling a clip with prepared media and narration, adjusting captions and exporting it. He also warns that this can consume substantial tokens. The demo illustrates delegated production, not a guarantee that every generated edit is accurate or economical.
