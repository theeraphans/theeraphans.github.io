Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models

Showed that few-shot examples containing intermediate reasoning steps can elicit large language models to solve multi-step arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic tasks more accurately.

The problem

What was missing before this work

Standard prompting asks a model to map a complex question directly to an answer. Multi-step tasks require intermediate variables and decomposition, but the output objective offers little room to compute them.

Earlier rationale methods often required training data or model changes. The paper tests a prompt-only method.

Core idea

The paper in one idea

Include worked examples whose outputs contain natural-language reasoning before the final answer. A sufficiently large language model imitates the pattern and generates intermediate steps for a new problem.

Those generated tokens act as a scratchpad, distributing computation across the sequence instead of demanding an immediate answer.

Prompt: question → rationale → answer; test: new question → generated rationale → answer.

Mechanism

How the method works

Choose representative demonstrations, write concise valid rationales, and format final answers consistently. Append a new question and sample or greedily decode the rationale and answer.

Score the final answer while separately inspecting reasoning errors. Multiple sampled chains can later be aggregated through self-consistency, though that is subsequent work.

Technical deep dive

The machinery that matters

The model’s recurrent computation occurs through autoregressive tokens: each generated step becomes context for the next. This increases inference tokens and exposes intermediate text to prompt and decoding choices.

The paper observed strong scale dependence. Small models often produced incoherent chains or failed to benefit, while very large models showed large gains.

Evidence

What the experiments established

On GSM8K, MultiArith, and other reasoning tasks, chain-of-thought examples substantially improved large-model accuracy; PaLM 540B with the method reached a new result on GSM8K at the time.

The evaluated tasks often have short, checkable answers. Gains and scale thresholds vary by model, prompt, and dataset.

Why it mattered

How it changed the field

Chain of thought changed prompting from answer formatting into inference-time computation and led to self-consistency, scratchpads, verifier-guided search, tool use, and reasoning-model training.

It also encouraged evaluation of process traces, though later work showed explanations can be plausible without faithfully reflecting why the model answered.

Engineering use

What an AI engineer should do with it

Use concise demonstrations that match the task and validate final outputs with deterministic tools where possible. Treat generated rationales as untrusted working state.

Measure accuracy against extra tokens, latency, and exposure risk. Hidden or summarized reasoning may be preferable when traces contain sensitive data or misleading explanations.

Limits and later corrections

Where the result stops

Chains can confidently contain invalid steps, rationalize an answer after the fact, or copy superficial prompt patterns. More tokens can compound error.

The method does not prove symbolic reasoning or faithful interpretability. Prompt selection and benchmark contamination can inflate results.

Takeaways

What to remember

  • Intermediate tokens can serve as an inference scratchpad.
  • Few-shot rationales teach a reasoning format without weight updates.
  • Benefits were strongly scale-dependent in the paper.
  • Final answers and reasoning validity need separate checks.
  • Generated explanations are not guaranteed faithful.
  • Extra reasoning trades latency and tokens for potential quality.
