Stanford CS329A · Self-Improving AI Agents

Evaluating Agents Beyond Short Tasks

A detailed guide to measuring time horizon, professional work products, research synthesis, reliability, and the failure modes that appear when agents work for longer.

Aakanksha ChowdheryVideo: 1h 15m 17sSummary run: September 15, 2026

Executive summary

Agent evaluation is no longer just question answering

This lecture asks how to evaluate agents that perform multi-step, economically useful work. A short-answer benchmark can check whether a model knows something. It says much less about whether an agent can plan, use tools, recover from mistakes, produce a professional artifact, or support every claim with evidence.

Aakanksha Chowdhery organizes the discussion around three benchmark families. METR estimates the duration of tasks an agent can complete at a specified reliability. GDPval compares model-produced work with deliverables from experienced professionals. DeepScholar-Bench evaluates literature-review generation through synthesis, retrieval, and citation verifiability.

METR

How difficult a task can the agent complete, using expert-human completion time as the scale?

GDPval

Can the model produce a realistic professional deliverable that graders prefer?

DeepScholar

Can a research agent retrieve, synthesize, and verifiably cite the literature?

No single result means that an agent can perform an entire occupation. Each benchmark isolates particular tasks under defined inputs, environments, and grading rules.

Why agent evals are harder

Success depends on trajectories, environments, and judgment

An agent does not merely emit one response. It chooses actions across time. It may inspect files, search, edit code, run tools, observe results, revise a plan, or stop too early. Evaluation therefore has to examine both the final outcome and whether the environment allows the task to be judged consistently.

Longer tasks introduce more places to fail. A small probability of error at each step compounds across a trajectory. The agent can misunderstand the environment, select the wrong tool, lose track of the objective, repeat an action, or decide it is finished before satisfying the requirements.

Human baselines are also difficult. Experts complete the same job at different speeds and may disagree about quality. The lecture discusses using several experts and checking inter-rater agreement, adding more judgments when estimates diverge substantially.

Evaluation is a measurement design problem.

A score is meaningful only when the task, starting state, tools, success criteria, human baseline, and reliability threshold are explicit.

METR time horizons

Measure task difficulty in human time, not agent runtime

METR's time horizon is commonly misunderstood. It does not mean an agent continuously operates for that many minutes. It is the human-expert completion time of tasks at which a fitted model predicts a chosen success probability.

To estimate it, researchers measure how long experts need for each task, run an agent on tasks of varying duration, and fit a curve relating task duration to success. The point where that curve reaches 50% is the 50%-time horizon. The same procedure can estimate an 80%-time horizon.

The task suite includes software and research-oriented activities ranging from finding a shell script or transforming JSON to debugging simulation inputs and optimizing CUDA kernels. Human duration acts as a rough proxy for difficulty, not as a perfect representation of all real work.

Capability and reliability are different axes

The lecture highlights a large gap between 50% and 80% success horizons. A model may occasionally complete a longer task while remaining dependable only on much shorter tasks. For a production system, the latter is often more important.

That distinction prevents an attractive maximum-capability demonstration from being mistaken for routine performance. A system that succeeds half the time still creates substantial review and recovery work.

What the trend does and does not show

The lecture discusses rapidly increasing task horizons across model generations. METR's current methodology fits a logistic success curve and reports both 50% and 80% horizons. Its own documentation warns that the suite's tasks are cleaner and more algorithmically scoreable than much real employment.

The curve therefore measures performance on the selected environment and task distribution. It does not prove that an agent can complete every task requiring the same amount of human time, nor that task horizons transfer directly to jobs involving relationships, organizational context, or ambiguous success criteria.

GDPval

Move from puzzles to professional deliverables

GDPval evaluates work products drawn from 44 occupations across nine economically significant U.S. industries. Tasks may require documents, spreadsheets, presentations, diagrams, multimedia, or other artifacts, with reference materials supplied as context.

The lecture gives examples such as identifying inconsistencies across purchase orders, designing a real-estate brochure, and optimizing a vendor-fair layout. These are more representative of professional output than exam questions, but their quality can also be subjective.

Win rate handles comparison, not absolute truth

Expert graders compare model output with a professional baseline. Structure, relevance, aesthetics, style, and correctness can all affect preference. Human graders may disagree, which is not noise to hide: it reveals that real work often lacks one indisputable answer.

The lecture reports substantial progress between model generations and discusses speed and cost advantages on tasks that models complete successfully. These figures must be read conditionally. They do not price the cost of every failure, all verification, or the organizational context required to specify the task.

Repeated attempts can improve results

Parallel or sequential sampling can give the system additional chances. In a sequential loop, later attempts use feedback from earlier output. This can improve a result, but it also changes cost, latency, and the evaluation protocol. A one-shot model and an iterative agent are not the same system.

A benchmark task is smaller than a job

Professionals decide what deserves attention, negotiate expectations, gather missing context, coordinate with people, and accept responsibility. GDPval asks models to create well-specified deliverables after context has been assembled.

Critical interpretation

Matching an expert on selected artifacts indicates automation potential at the task level. It does not establish occupation-level replacement, because the benchmark does not capture every activity or the process of discovering what work should be done.

DeepScholar-Bench

A literature review must retrieve, synthesize, and prove its claims

DeepScholar-Bench uses recent research papers to create a live research-synthesis task: generate a related-work section by finding prior work, explaining relationships, and citing evidence.

It evaluates three dimensions. Knowledge synthesis asks whether the response covers and connects the important ideas. Retrieval quality asks whether it found the relevant papers. Verifiability asks whether citations actually support the claims attached to them.

This decomposition matters because fluent writing can conceal weak research. A report may read coherently while omitting central work, citing peripheral sources, or attaching a real citation to a claim that the source does not support.

Why one metric is insufficient

Retrieving many papers does not guarantee understanding. Producing a broad narrative does not guarantee evidence. Correct individual citations do not guarantee adequate coverage of the field. Research synthesis requires all three dimensions to work together.

The primary paper reports that the benchmark remains far from saturated. The lecture similarly emphasizes poor quality at longer horizons: an agent may remain active for a substantial task without creating an academically trustworthy output.

Because the benchmark draws from recent papers and live retrieval, it also aims to reduce staleness and memorization problems found in static question-answering sets. That makes the environment more realistic, but it does not remove the need for expert review of literature claims.

Reading the benchmarks together

Duration, economic value, and research quality are complementary

METR tells us about success as task difficulty grows. GDPval tells us about comparative quality on professional work products. DeepScholar-Bench examines a specialized workflow where retrieval and citation integrity are part of correctness.

A research-synthesis task can appear long in a time-horizon evaluation yet score poorly on evidence coverage. A polished GDPval artifact can win a comparison while relying on a prompt that already contains context a professional would normally need to discover. A reliable short task may still have little economic value.

Can it continue?

Long-horizon capability and recovery across a trajectory.

Is the artifact useful?

Professional preference, correctness, format, and relevance.

Can we trust the basis?

Coverage, source retrieval, and claim-level verification.

A serious evaluation program should choose dimensions from the real failure cost of its application instead of adopting one benchmark because it is popular.

Failure modes

Long tasks expose weaknesses that short demos hide

  • Poor planning: the agent starts acting without decomposing the objective or identifying dependencies.
  • Wrong tool selection: it has a relevant capability but invokes an unsuitable tool or supplies incorrect arguments.
  • Weak environment understanding: it misunderstands files, code, state, or constraints.
  • Premature abandonment: it interprets an obstacle as a reason to stop rather than diagnose and recover.
  • Repetitive loops: it repeats an action without learning from the unchanged result.
  • Local success, global failure: an intermediate step works, but the final artifact does not satisfy the original task.
  • Unreliable evidence: generated research sounds plausible while retrieval or citation support is incomplete.

Memory may improve understanding when an agent repeatedly works in the same codebase or environment. It is not an automatic cure: stale or incorrect memory can introduce a new failure source.

What to capture in an evaluation

Store the starting state, prompt, tool definitions, complete trajectory, produced artifacts, final state, cost, latency, and grader result. Recording only the final prose makes planning and tool failures difficult to diagnose.

Test repeated trials when reliability matters. Report the success distribution rather than selecting the best-looking run. A system intended for independent action needs a stricter reliability target than one whose output is always reviewed by an expert.

What this means for work

Capability growth is real; job-level conclusions need restraint

The lecture treats professional benchmarks as a useful taxonomy of tasks that models may perform. This is more informative than broad speculation, because it lets teams identify specific deliverables, contexts, and measurable outcomes.

But workplace performance includes prioritization, tacit knowledge, accountability, interaction, and deciding which problem matters. When all relevant context is written into a prompt, a model may execute strongly. Converting unspoken organizational knowledge into that specification is itself work.

Chowdhery describes current systems as useful scientific brainstorming partners rather than independent AI scientists that formulate hypotheses, conduct experiments, and complete the entire discovery loop reliably. Her largest reservation is the long tail of reliability.

The practical conclusion is not to ignore progress or assume immediate full replacement. It is to decompose roles into tasks, evaluate those tasks honestly, redesign workflows around demonstrated strengths, and preserve human review where failures remain costly or difficult to detect.

Practical takeaways

How to evaluate your own agent

  1. Define a real deliverable and the environment in which it must be produced.
  2. Collect several representative tasks, including messy and adversarial cases.
  3. Write success criteria before running the agent.
  4. Establish human baselines for time and quality, noting disagreement.
  5. Capture trajectories and tool calls, not only final answers.
  6. Run enough trials to estimate reliability rather than showcase one success.
  7. Score the dimensions that matter separately: completion, quality, evidence, cost, and latency.
  8. Investigate failures by class: planning, tools, environment, loops, stopping, or verification.
  9. Re-evaluate after changing the model, prompt, toolset, memory, or retry policy.
  10. State clearly what the benchmark does not represent.
