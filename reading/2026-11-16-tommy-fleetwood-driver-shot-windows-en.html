Three Driver Windows, One Club

Tommy Fleetwood does not treat driver as a single maximum-speed swing. He changes the task around the ball to produce a fairway finder, a stock drive, or a high-launch draw, then judges each shot by the window it was designed to fill.

Executive summary

Fleetwood changes the environment before changing the swing

Fleetwood describes driver as a versatile scoring club. He wants the speed to move the ball, but he also wants a shot he trusts on a narrow finishing hole. His answer is not to force one swing to cover every situation. He changes tee height, stance, alignment, address position, and visual task so the required delivery becomes easier to organize.

The clearest example is his fairway finder. He keeps his normal tee height, opens and narrows the stance, hovers the club, and places a second tee behind the ball. His only job is to swing over that rear tee. The constraint discourages him from moving too far behind the ball and helps produce a more downward, controlled delivery. The shot fades, carries less, spins more, and releases after landing.

For a high-launch draw, he reverses the picture. The tee can sit higher, he allows himself to get more behind the ball, and he gives the ball room to start right before turning back. The point is not that every player needs Fleetwood's three shapes. It is that each shape has a setup, start-line window, and measurable result.

Fleetwood calibrates these windows on the range. After hitting the controlled and high versions, the stock shot sits between two known extremes. That is more useful than warming up with ten identical drivers and hoping the course asks for the same flight.

Window 1 · Fairway finder

Keep tee height constant and change the task

A common safety shot lowers the tee and catches the ball low on the face. Fleetwood explains that this can add spin and hold the flight, but it also changes strike conditions. His newer version keeps the tee at its normal height. Fewer variables need to be recalibrated under pressure.

He opens the stance, makes it slightly narrower, and hovers the driver. The open stance matches the intended fade and gives him a start-line window up the left. Hovering helps him feel stable during the takeaway. The narrower base and open alignment appear as natural responses to the task rather than positions he tries to copy one by one.

The constraint drill

A rear tee turns six swing thoughts into one external task

Fleetwood places a second tee a short distance behind the ball and raises it high enough to become a visible obstacle. He then hovers the driver and swings over it. If he misses the tee, he has probably avoided moving the club and body too far behind the ball.

The drill resembles a ball-first iron-strike exercise, but Fleetwood warns that driver will not descend as much as the exaggerated picture suggests. The task simply stops him from hanging too far back. His summary is refreshingly concrete: if he misses the tee, he has done what he wanted in the swing.

Make the drill safe. Begin with the rear tee low and far enough behind the ball that contact is unlikely. Use slow rehearsal swings before adding speed. The tee is feedback, not something to challenge aggressively with the clubhead.

What changes in the flight

The safer driver trades carry for a controlled landing pattern

For the filmed fairway-finder shot, the presenters report an attack angle around 3 degrees down, 265 yards of carry, roughly 3,000 rpm of spin, and 172 mph of ball speed. Fleetwood says the carry is shorter, but the ball runs after landing. The total distance penalty is smaller than the carry number alone implies.

The key trade is not simply distance versus accuracy. It is carry, spin, curve, and rollout together. A low fade can be excellent on a firm fairway with trouble left. It may be a poor choice when a bunker requires maximum carry or when the ground is soft. The shot window must match the hole.

Window 2 · Stock driver

The middle flight becomes easier to feel after both extremes

Fleetwood uses the low controlled shot and the high-launch shot as calibration points. Once he has produced both, he says he can stand up and find what square feels like between them. This is a useful warm-up design: learn the edges of the available motion, then settle into the center.

This prevents the stock swing from becoming a vague command to "swing normal." Normal now has neighbors. If the player starts launching everything too high, the fairway-finder task can pull the delivery back. If the flight becomes flat and cutty, the high-tee window can restore the opposite feel.

Window 3 · High draw

Reverse the environment and give the curve room

To create the opposite shot, Fleetwood raises the tee, allows himself to get more behind the ball, and lets the club release. The intended ball begins right and returns left. Alignment matters because a draw needs space. Setting up square to the final target and trying to manufacture curve can produce the double-cross he wants to avoid.

Course strategy

Play the shape you can repeat before trying to own every shape

Asked for one tip for a high-handicap player, Fleetwood first mentions driver fitting. Then he gives a more important course lesson: identify the shot that already appears most often and use it. A repeatable fade or draw is more useful than a theoretically perfect straight ball that appears once every ten swings.

Professionals know their current limits and stay inside them during competition. They practice a missing shot before taking it to the course. That is the opposite of using the first tee as a laboratory. A player can build a three-window system gradually, beginning with the stock pattern and one safer variation.

  • Tight hole: choose the window with the most predictable start line and curve, even if it carries less.
  • Forced carry: favor the window that reliably clears the obstacle, not the one with the best total rollout.
  • Wind: match launch and spin to the condition rather than labeling one trajectory as always superior.
  • Unpracticed curve: do not take it to the course because the hole seems to request it.

The over-the-ball routine

Finish the decisions first, then keep one swing thought

Fleetwood says his best golf usually includes one swing thought. It can change from day to day. Sometimes it concerns transition, impact, or staying centered. The important part is that shot selection, club choice, target, and visualization are complete before he addresses the ball.

The single thought gives him something he can execute and evaluate. Even when the result is imperfect, he can tell whether the intended transition or impact picture occurred. Five technical commands would make that review impossible because no one knows which command changed the shot.

Useful sequence: choose the shot window, choose the start line, picture the curve, make one rehearsal, then carry one cue into the swing. The cue supports the decision instead of replacing it.

Strike calibration

Keep ball position and the center of the turn observable

Fleetwood identifies low-face contact as his common driver miss. He connects it to hanging back or swaying away from the ball and failing to return. His two calibration points are ball position and a centered head while the body makes a full turn.

He may ask his coach to hold a club or alignment stick beside his head. The reference can sit on either side depending on the day's tendency. The intent is not to freeze the head. Fleetwood explicitly pairs "centered" with a full turn. A rigid head that restricts rotation solves the wrong problem.

Ball position offers another constant. Fleetwood mentions the left heel or just inside it as a repeatable reference, while acknowledging that conditions and desired flights can move the ball. A constant is valuable because it makes a strike change easier to diagnose. If both ball position and body motion wander, the player cannot tell which variable caused the low-face contact.

A 40-minute practice

Build the windows without copying Fleetwood's exact positions

  1. Map the stock shot, 10 minutes. Hit eight drivers with one tee height and one target. Record face strike, start line, curve, and carry.
  2. Test the rear-tee constraint, 10 minutes. Rehearse slowly, then hit five shots while missing the obstacle. Compare the flight with the stock group.
  3. Add a safe window, 8 minutes. Open the stance only enough to match a playable fade. Give the start line room and keep the same tee height.
  4. Explore the opposite window, 8 minutes. Raise the tee slightly and create room for a draw. Stop if strike or start line becomes unpredictable.
  5. Choose one course shot, 4 minutes. Finish with three balls using the window you would trust on a narrow first tee.

Keep the change only if the evidence improves. A pretty rehearsal or a position that resembles Fleetwood does not matter when strike dispersion, start line, or carry becomes worse.

My take

The genius is the task design, not a secret body position

The most transferable idea in this video is Fleetwood's use of constraints. He does not need to calculate every joint angle required for a fairway finder. A rear tee, a start-line window, and one instruction organize the motion around a visible job.

His three driver windows also connect technique to strategy. Each swing exists because the course asks a different question. That keeps experimentation honest. A lower fade is valuable when it improves the hole, not because "hitting down" is inherently better. A high draw earns its place when the player can start it safely and use the extra carry.

For someone trying to learn from Fleetwood, copy the decision architecture first: define the shot, alter the environment, use one cue, and verify the flight. His exact motion belongs to his body, equipment, speed, and history.
