Economic modeling · Labor · Automation · Distribution · 2030 scenarios

Three Economic Futures for Transformative AI

Anthropic Institute models how assumptions about capability, adoption, autonomy, productivity, new tasks, and worker adjustment could reshape US growth, unemployment, wages, and the balance between labor and capital by 2030.

Anthropic Institute · Korinek et al.57-page working paperVersion 1.0 · September 2026Summary run September 10, 2026 · 20:32 ICT

This article combines Anthropic's interactive explainer with its companion technical report, Working Paper 2026-02. The authors explicitly say the scenarios are not predictions and assign them no probabilities. Results are conditional outputs of a simplified model, not forecasts endorsed by external reviewers.

Executive summary

The economy can become richer while many workers become worse off

The report asks a conditional question: if AI reaches a given capability level, spreads at a given speed, automates rather than augments a given share of work, and creates a given number of new human tasks, what follows for the US economy by 2030?

Across all three scenarios, output rises. The disagreement is about scale and distribution. In the modest case, AI resembles a historically important general technology whose macroeconomic effects arrive gradually. In the substantial case, growth roughly doubles from its normal pace, knowledge-worker wages stagnate, and employment shifts toward less-exposed occupations. In the extreme case, recursively improving and rapidly adopted AI produces unprecedented growth, while knowledge work contracts sharply, unemployment exceeds typical recessionary levels, and capital receives a much larger share of national income.

The core result

GDP is not a welfare dashboard. A larger economy can coexist with occupational displacement, falling relative wages, prolonged unemployment, and a transfer of income from workers to owners of capital. The more transformative AI becomes, the more distribution—not production—becomes the central policy problem.

How the model works

The economy is represented as millions of task instances

Each occupation is a bundle of tasks derived from the US Department of Labor's O*NET taxonomy. A nurse, for example, checks patients, draws blood, records vitals, writes discharge instructions, schedules care, and orders supplies. Some tasks remain human, some are augmented, some are automated, and technological change creates new tasks.

1 · CapabilityWhich tasks can AI affect?

More capable systems reach more difficult cognitive work.

2 · AdoptionHow widely is AI used?

Technical feasibility does not imply immediate diffusion.

3 · ModeAugment or automate?

AI either raises human productivity or substitutes capital for labor.

4 · RenewalAre new tasks created?

New human work can offset displacement from automated tasks.

5 · AdjustmentHow quickly can workers move?

Search friction and occupation switching determine unemployment.

At the task-instance level, labor and capital compete to provide output. Across different tasks, they are modeled as complements: bottleneck tasks still matter. AI also contributes to innovation by increasing research input, but the model uses a semi-endogenous growth structure and retains physical bottlenecks. Consequently, research automation adds relatively little to growth before 2030—a result the authors call a likely lower bound.

Three scenarios

Modest, Substantial, and Extreme encode different technology paths

ModestHistorically familiar change

AI's effect is comparable to the internet: real but gradual gains, limited macro disruption, and labor outcomes close to historical ranges.

SubstantialA knowledge-work revolution

AI can perform half of knowledge work by 2030, much of it autonomously, but adoption remains incomplete. Growth accelerates while knowledge-worker wages flatten.

ExtremeEconomic transformation

AI outperforms humans on most knowledge tasks, performs nearly all autonomously, creates almost no new knowledge tasks, and diffuses rapidly—likely requiring recursive self-improvement.

In the extreme case, annual GDP growth reaches roughly 15%, implying economic doubling in about 4.5 years. Nearly one in five cognitive workers is unemployed in the technical paper's scenario. These numbers do not describe what the authors think will happen; they describe what follows if the extreme assumptions hold together.

Finding one

AI raises output in every scenario

Automation lowers the cost of affected task instances, while augmentation makes workers more productive. Both channels increase output. Wider adoption and stronger productivity effects generate larger gains. The substantial case puts 2030 GDP about 8.3% above a counterfactual economy without AI; the extreme case puts it 32.4% higher.

The counterfactual matters. These figures do not mean the economy grows only 1.6%, 8.3%, or 32.4% from today. They measure the additional level of GDP relative to where the model says it would have been without AI, at 2025 prices.

Finding two

Displacement becomes unemployment when switching occupations is slow

As AI substitutes for cognitive labor, employment demand shifts toward occupations modeled as less directly exposed, such as nursing, construction, and electrical work. A software engineer cannot instantly become an electrician. Workers may need credentials, training, relocation, lower starting wages, or a complete identity change.

The model represents this through search-and-matching friction and lower search effectiveness across occupational groups. More displacement means more people between jobs. In modest and most substantial paths, aggregate unemployment remains within historical experience. In the extreme path, the volume and speed of reallocation overwhelm normal adjustment and unemployment rises beyond ordinary recessionary levels.

Aggregate stability can hide personal damage

The model does not follow individual workers and gives all workers within each broad group the same wage. It therefore cannot represent lost tenure, geography, age, caregiving constraints, firm-specific skills, or unequal retraining access. Its unemployment result is macroeconomic; lived displacement may be much harsher.

Finding three

Average wages can rise while knowledge-worker wages fall

AI increases productivity and expands demand for complementary physical work. Faster design and permitting, for example, could increase construction activity and wages. At the same time, reduced demand for human cognitive tasks puts downward pressure on knowledge-work wages.

In the substantial scenario, knowledge-worker wages are essentially flat relative to the no-AI path while other workers gain. In the extreme scenario, cognitive wages are 11.5% below their counterfactual path and wages elsewhere are about 34% above it. Average wage growth therefore does not mean every group benefits.

Finding four

The larger pie shifts toward capital in the medium run

Today the model begins with roughly 60 cents of each dollar of output going to labor and 40 cents to capital. AI makes compute and other capital more productive. Between now and 2030, capital supply cannot adjust infinitely quickly, so owners receive higher returns.

The modeled labor share falls from 60% to 59.4% in the modest case, 56.1% in the substantial case, and 45.2% in the extreme case. In the extreme scenario, total labor income is barely changed despite a much larger economy. Society produces far more, but ownership determines who captures the gain.

Distributional pressure

Policies that increase broad capital ownership, worker bargaining power, social insurance, or access to AI-enhanced production address a different problem from policies that merely accelerate adoption. Growth and distribution require separate instruments.

Public expectations

Median US beliefs resemble the Substantial scenario

In August 2026, Morning Consult surveyed 10,980 US adults about eight capability milestones, adoption, autonomy, productivity, and the time required to find new work. The median answers imply an economy close to the substantial path: GDP around 8% higher by 2030 and cognitive employment about 4% lower than without AI.

The public-facing explorer also compares responses with site visitors, but that group is self-selected and should not be treated as representative. Around 10% of general-public respondents express beliefs aligned with the extreme scenario. Wide disagreement is itself an important result: economic preparation cannot rely on one socially shared timeline.

Model limitations

What the explorer deliberately leaves outside the frame

No roboticsPhysical work remains unexposed.

Rapid advances in robots could weaken the modeled refuge of manual occupations and alter wages across both groups.

No policy responsesInstitutions do not react.

Taxes, transfers, regulation, education, labor rules, and public investment could materially change every outcome.

No business cyclesDemand and finance stay quiet.

The model omits recessions, financial disruption, price rigidities, and feedback where job loss depresses spending.

Coarse workersTwo occupation groups hide inequality.

Tenure, skill, region, demographics, firms, and transition losses are not modeled at useful human resolution.

Simplified capitalCompute is not separated.

One capital good and an exogenous supply schedule omit saving decisions and important infrastructure constraints.

Limited innovation loopResearch acceleration stays conservative.

The framework cannot generate an explosive singularity and may understate feedback from research into further automation.

No household productionUseful activity outside GDP disappears.

AI help with care, learning, planning, and household work may improve welfare without appearing in measured output.

No catastrophic riskOnly ordinary economic channels remain.

Severe safety failures and political-economy breakdowns sit outside all three scenarios.

External economists reviewed an early draft but were not asked to endorse the conclusions. Some thought exposed occupations might grow rather than shrink. Some viewed the extreme case as a thought experiment, while others thought the modest case already understated observed change. These disagreements are part of the model's proper use, not footnotes to ignore.

How to read it

The explorer is a translation layer between AI beliefs and economic beliefs

The model's real contribution is comparability. Debates about AI often mix capability timelines, adoption rates, automation shares, productivity multipliers, and labor-market flexibility. Two people may appear to disagree about unemployment while actually assuming different adoption speeds or different rates of new-task creation.

By making five assumptions explicit, the explorer lets users see which technological belief produces which economic consequence. It does not identify the correct input values. A user can generate a precise chart from poorly grounded assumptions; interactive precision should not be mistaken for predictive confidence.

Implications

What leaders should monitor before 2030

  1. Task-level capability: which economically important tasks AI can complete at required quality, not just benchmark scores.
  2. Adoption: actual usage across firms and task instances, including organizational bottlenecks.
  3. Augmentation versus automation: whether deployments expand worker output or remove the worker from production.
  4. New-task creation: the rate, quality, and accessibility of genuinely new human work.
  5. Occupation switching: time to reemployment, wage loss, credentials, geography, and who bears retraining cost.
  6. Wage divergence: exposed occupations versus physical and complementary work.
  7. Labor share: whether productivity growth reaches paychecks or accumulates primarily as capital returns.
  8. Capital access: ownership of compute, AI firms, data, and complementary infrastructure.
  9. AI-enabled research: evidence that faster discovery feeds back into capability and productivity.
  10. Robotics: whether physical occupations remain a durable destination for displaced workers.

Critical assessment

A valuable map of assumptions, not a map of destiny

StrongTasks are better than job labels.

Occupations change internally before they disappear, and adoption happens instance by instance.

StrongDistribution is modeled explicitly.

GDP, wages, unemployment, and factor shares reveal different kinds of success and harm.

StrongAdjustment friction is central.

The report does not assume displaced workers immediately find equivalent work.

WeaknessTwo worker groups are extremely coarse.

The simplification limits conclusions about sectors, places, demographics, and individual welfare.

WeaknessThe 2030 horizon truncates adaptation.

It captures medium-run scarcity and disruption but not eventual capital accumulation or institutional change.

Conflict contextAnthropic studies an economy its products may reshape.

The transparent methods and external comments help, but assumptions and framing still deserve independent replication.

My conclusion: use the framework to make disagreements legible and to design indicators, stress tests, and policy triggers. Do not cite one scenario number as “Anthropic predicts.” The authors specifically designed the paper to avoid that reading.

Takeaways

Twelve ideas worth keeping

  1. The scenarios are not predictions and carry no assigned probabilities.
  2. Jobs are bundles of changing tasks, not indivisible units.
  3. Capability, adoption, and autonomy are distinct variables.
  4. Augmentation and automation have different labor consequences.
  5. New human tasks can offset displacement, but their rate is uncertain.
  6. Switching occupations creates friction and prolonged unemployment.
  7. GDP can rise while knowledge-worker wages stagnate or fall.
  8. Average wages can conceal sharp divergence between groups.
  9. Capital can capture a growing share of a much larger economy.
  10. Median public expectations resemble the substantial-change case.
  11. Robotics and policy responses are major omissions.
  12. The extreme scenario turns distribution into the main economic challenge.
