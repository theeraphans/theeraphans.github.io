Five Tour Habits That Make Golf More Predictable

Tommy Fleetwood's lesson is less about copying a tour swing than improving five decisions: how you deliver driver and irons, how you describe a chip, how you map club distances, and what you remember after the round.

9:16 videoRick Shiels Golf · Tommy FleetwoodPublished December 23, 2021Summary run: November 13, 2026 · 09:30 ICT

Executive summary

The five tips solve five different kinds of uncertainty

Rick Shiels asks Fleetwood for five things tour players do better than most amateurs. Fleetwood does not answer with one model swing. He moves through five separate problems: driver delivery, iron contact, chip planning, distance control, and post-round thinking.

The first two tips separate the driver from an iron. A teed driver can benefit from a more upward strike, while a useful iron shot needs pressure moving toward the lead side so the club contacts ball before turf. The drills exaggerate those differences. A forward tee gives the driver a visible obstacle to miss. A lead-foot step forces the iron swing to move through the ball instead of hanging back.

The other three tips are decision tools. Fleetwood describes a chip before playing it, records several repeatable distances for each club, and ends every review by recalling three good shots. Together they reduce the number of choices that must be invented while standing over the ball.

Tip 1 · Driver

The forward-tee drill teaches an upward delivery without a technical lecture

Fleetwood starts with the physical difference between driver and the rest of the bag. The ball is teed up, so the player can meet it while the clubhead is travelling upward. Many amateurs keep the same downward intention they use for an iron. In Fleetwood's explanation, that delivery adds spin and often produces a weak cut.

His setup cue is simple. Move the ball forward, let the trail foot sit a little farther back, and keep the head behind the ball. He then places a second tee in the ground ahead of the ball. The task is to strike the ball and let the driver travel above the forward tee. A player who chops steeply downward is more likely to hit it.

The drill is useful because it changes the task rather than asking the player to manufacture a launch-monitor number. It still needs feedback. A very upward strike can create a new problem if contact moves low on the face or the path and face no longer match. Spray the face, watch the start line, and compare carry and curvature before keeping the change.

Tip 2 · Irons

Use the ground to move pressure forward and let the loft lift the ball

The iron lesson reverses the driver intention. Fleetwood wants the club to reach the ball before the turf. The loft already supplies height. Trying to help the ball upward often leaves pressure on the trail foot and moves the bottom of the swing behind the ball, producing fat and thin contact.

Fleetwood exaggerates the correction. He lifts the lead foot during the backswing, then stamps it down as the downswing begins. The motion shifts pressure toward the target and gives the body somewhere to rotate. He can then strike down through the ball and take the divot after impact.

Contact tells you whether the exercise worked. Mark the ball position on a grass range or use a line on a mat. The strike should occur at the ball and the low point should appear beyond it. If the player simply lunges toward the target, the drill has lost its purpose. Pressure should move forward while the chest continues turning.

Tip 3 · Chipping

Build the shot in words before trying to build it with the club

Fleetwood's chipping advice begins away from the ball. He reads the slope, checks the lie, chooses a club, identifies a landing point, and predicts the release. In the filmed example he uses a 52-degree wedge, lands the ball just onto the green, and expects it to move from right to left.

He describes the motion as a putt with loft. The phrase connects an unfamiliar little wedge shot to a familiar putting action. More importantly, it gives the shot one positive picture. The alternative is a pile of warnings about what must not happen, which leaves the player with no useful target.

  1. Read the lie. Decide how cleanly the club can reach the ball and how much spin is realistic.
  2. Read the green. Identify the first slope the ball will meet after landing.
  3. Choose a trajectory. Pick the club and amount of loft that can reach the landing area.
  4. Name one landing point. Make it small enough to aim at, then predict the roll from there.
  5. Review both parts. Separate a poor landing from a poor read. They require different practice.

Tip 4 · Distance control

A club needs a repeatable range, not one flattering number

Tour players know more than a single stock distance. They can shorten a swing, reduce speed, or choose another flight. Fleetwood asks amateurs to start with something even more basic: measure what the club actually does across several shots.

A player may call an 8-iron a 164-yard club because one perfect strike once travelled that far. The next group of shots might carry 142, 149, and 153. Those numbers are not failures to hide. They describe the club the player takes to the course. A front bunker or water carry should be planned against the reliable part of that range, not the best result in memory.

Fleetwood also makes the scoring geometry concrete. A ball finishing 40 feet short and another finishing 40 feet long are 80 feet apart. Reaching pin-high more often narrows that error before direction is considered. This is why under-clubbing costs more than a single missed green suggests.

Club selection

Use carry distance to clear trouble, then let the target decide the club

The distance lesson becomes a risk lesson when water or a bunker guards the green. Fleetwood says tour players will take more club when the required carry sits at the edge of the expected number. They do not need to prove they can hit the shorter club perfectly.

For an amateur, the same idea starts with honest carry data. Total distance is misleading when the ball must clear an obstacle. Build a small note for each club with typical carry, a softer carry, and the shortest result you still see often enough to matter. On the course, compare the hazard number with that range before looking at the flag.

A practical range session. Hit eight normal shots with one club. Remove obvious mishits only if you would also treat them as unusual on the course. Record carry, strike location, and curve. Use the middle group as the stock range and the shorter recurring outcomes when planning a forced carry.

Tip 5 · Post-round review

Leave the course with one practice task and three shots worth keeping

Fleetwood reviews the round after he plays. He looks at what worked, what failed, and what should change before the next start. That analysis has a purpose: it should produce the next practice task. Replaying every mistake without choosing an action only extends the frustration.

He finishes with a second exercise. Recall the three best shots of the day. The point is not forced optimism. Golf makes failures unusually memorable, and those memories can distort how the player evaluates the whole round. Three specific successes preserve evidence of what the swing and decisions could already produce.

A useful note can be four lines long: the shot that cost the most, the likely cause, the next drill, and the three best shots. This keeps practice connected to the course while preventing one bad hole from becoming the story of the entire day.

A 45-minute practice

Turn the video into one measured session

  1. Driver, 10 minutes. Use the forward-tee drill for three balls, then remove the obstacle for three balls. Compare strike and flight. Repeat once.
  2. Irons, 10 minutes. Alternate one lead-foot step swing with one normal swing. Mark low point and keep the same target.
  3. Chipping, 10 minutes. Play five different lies. Say the club, landing point, and expected break before every shot.
  4. Distance map, 10 minutes. Hit eight normal shots with one scoring club. Record every carry and identify the range you would trust over trouble.
  5. Review, 5 minutes. Write one adjustment for the next session and the three best shots from this one.

The session preserves the video's central idea. A technical cue should produce observable evidence. A course decision should use a measured range. A review should change the next practice session.

My take

The best advice is the part that reduces invention under pressure

The driver and iron drills will attract the most attention because they change visible movement. The stronger lesson runs through the last three tips. Fleetwood prepares the decision before the swing. The chip has a written description, the club has a measured range, and the round ends with a defined next step.

That preparation matters because a golf shot offers too many possible corrections. A player who reaches the ball with a landing point, a reliable carry range, and one swing cue has fewer arguments to settle during the routine. The goal is not to think less about golf. It is to move the useful thinking to a time when the club is not already in your hands.
