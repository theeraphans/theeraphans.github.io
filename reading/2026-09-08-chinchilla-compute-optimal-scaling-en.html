Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models

Re-estimated compute-optimal scaling and showed that many large language models were undertrained: model parameters and training tokens should grow roughly together.

The problem

What was missing before this work

Labs were increasing parameter count faster than training data, following earlier scaling prescriptions. Under a fixed FLOPs budget, an oversized model trained on too few tokens may stop before its parameters are used effectively.

The study revisits the allocation question with many runs and four complementary estimation approaches.

Core idea

The paper in one idea

For compute-optimal training, scale model size and token count at approximately equal rates. A smaller model trained on substantially more data can outperform a larger undertrained model while using the same training compute.

DeepMind validates the prescription by training Chinchilla, a 70B-parameter model on 1.4 trillion tokens, and comparing it with much larger models.

Compute-optimal rule of thumb in the studied regime: training tokens ≈ 20 × parameters.

Mechanism

How the method works

Train families of models while varying parameters and tokens under controlled budgets. Fit loss surfaces and derive the parameter/data combination that minimizes loss for each compute level.

Build a model near the predicted optimum, then evaluate it on language modeling and downstream tasks against larger baselines such as Gopher.

Technical deep dive

The machinery that matters

Training compute is approximated from parameters and tokens, but constant factors and architecture details matter. The loss surface separates capacity from data exposure and reveals undertraining as a resource-allocation error.

The famous token-to-parameter ratio is a useful summary, not a law of nature. Repeated data, data quality, optimizer changes, sparsity, and multimodal training alter the frontier.

Evidence

What the experiments established

Chinchilla matched or outperformed much larger models on a wide set of tasks while requiring less inference memory. Its held-out loss agreed with the study’s compute-optimal predictions.

The comparison demonstrates a better allocation for the tested regime. It does not mean 70B is universally optimal or that every dataset can be scaled without quality degradation.

Why it mattered

How it changed the field

The result redirected the field toward more tokens per parameter and exposed data supply as a frontier constraint. Later model families trained smaller architectures longer, improving deployment economics as well as quality.

It also clarified that model size is a poor proxy for training investment and capability without token count and data information.

Engineering use

What an AI engineer should do with it

Plan training with loss-versus-compute sweeps, then include expected inference volume. A smaller, better-trained model may cost more to pretrain but much less to serve over its lifetime.

Track unique and effective tokens, not just a nominal count. Excessive repetition can violate assumptions behind scaling estimates and increase memorization.

Limits and later corrections

Where the result stops

The estimates depend on dense Transformer training, the selected corpus, and loss objective. Later work debates optimal ratios under higher-quality data, repeated epochs, and different compute accounting.

Compute-optimal pretraining does not automatically optimize instruction following, factuality, context length, or post-training behavior.

Connections

Where this paper sits in the learning path

Chinchilla directly corrects the allocation implied by the 2020 scaling-laws paper. LLaMA applies the deployment-friendly lesson of training smaller models on more data. Test-Time Scaling later adds inference compute to the allocation problem.

Takeaways

What to remember

  • Many early large models were undertrained for their parameter count.
  • Parameters and tokens should be scaled together in the studied regime.
  • A smaller trained-longer model can beat a larger model at equal FLOPs.
  • Training-optimal and deployment-optimal are different objectives.
  • Token quality and uniqueness remain constraints.
  • Report parameters, tokens, and compute together.
