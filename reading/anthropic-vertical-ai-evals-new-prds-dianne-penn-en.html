Vertical AI, Evals, And Frontier Products

A detailed reading guide to Lenny Rachitsky's conversation with Dianne Penn, Head of Product for Anthropic's AI Research and Labs teams, on why model progress needs product surfaces, why evals are becoming the new PRDs, and how user feedback becomes model improvement.

Source: Lenny's Podcast Guest: Dianne Penn, Anthropic Length: about 93 minutes Transcript: 2,359 caption rows, about 16,377 words Requested timestamp: 29:12

Executive Summary

This interview is one of the most useful product-management conversations in the library because it explains how a frontier AI lab turns fuzzy user feedback into model, harness, and product improvements. Dianne Penn's main lesson is that AI product work is no longer only about writing a PRD and shipping a feature. It is about discovering capabilities, building the right product surface, measuring failures as evals, and shortening the path from user pain to research action.

  • Frontier models need frontier products: Opus 4.5 and Claude Code amplified each other. A powerful model needs a product vehicle that lets users feel the new capability.
  • Capabilities arrive discontinuously: models may suddenly become good at a new behavior. Teams need evals and hands-on exploration to notice the jump.
  • Evals are the new PRDs: for AI products, the defining artifact is often an eval set that captures a real user pain, not a long requirements document.
  • Sweat the tokens: PMs need to inspect failed trajectories, tool calls, retrieved facts, schema failures, and model behavior with the same care older product teams gave to pixels.
  • Feedback must become actionable: "Claude hallucinated" is not useful enough. The product job is to diagnose whether the issue is tool use, search, synthesis, alignment, context, or another component.
  • Human judgment remains central: AI can be a thinking partner, but leaders still need taste, persistence, point of view, and responsibility for what gets shipped.

Core Thesis

The title says AI is going vertical again, but the deeper point is that value is moving from generic model capability into specific product surfaces, workflows, and feedback loops. Anthropic's early challenge was not only to train strong models. It had to find where Claude created distinctive user value, then build the product paths that made those capabilities legible.

Penn describes Anthropic as moving from a small team with one API engineer and a chat assistant into a company that found its identity through research demos, coding, tools, computer use, MCP, Skills, Claude Code, Claude Tag, and Labs. The pattern is not "model first, product later." It is a loop: research creates possible capability, product exposes it to users, users reveal where it works or fails, and evals turn that evidence back into research and product work.

Best one-line read: the winning AI product loop is capability discovery plus product surface plus eval feedback plus research improvement.

Anthropic's Early Product Identity

Dianne joined Anthropic in 2023, when the product engineering team was tiny and Claude was not yet strongly associated with coding. She says the culture, mission, and bottom-up energy were already present, but the company was still discovering how the technology would bring value to users and society.

One surprising example is Golden Gate Claude. Anthropic published interpretability research showing that model "features" could be identified and adjusted. The team turned that research into a 24-hour public demo where Claude became unusually focused on the Golden Gate Bridge. The demo reached a modest number of users, but internally it showed that Anthropic could turn research into a user experience quickly, authentically, and with product, design, engineering, and research collaborating in startup mode.

Golden Gate Claude matters here because it was a culture signal: research should not stay trapped in papers when a simple product experience can help people feel what was discovered.

Inflection Points: Opus, Coding, And Claude Code

Penn names Opus 3 as an internal confidence-building moment. It proved Anthropic could train and ship a frontier model, and it helped the company answer why users should choose Claude. A smaller but important training choice around long-form code generation also helped Claude stand out among early developers, because users were beginning to move beyond autocomplete into larger coding tasks.

She then describes Opus 4.5 and Claude Code as a mutual acceleration. Opus 4.5 would not have had the same moment without Claude Code as a product vehicle, and Claude Code would not have accelerated the same way without a model strong enough for end-to-end agentic coding. This is the "frontier products for frontier models" thesis.

Living Inside The Exponential

The interview spends time on what it feels like when model progress is accelerating. Penn says teams cannot perfectly predict which model release will unlock which capability. That means the product organization must be adaptable, first-principles driven, and willing to change plans when new evidence appears.

She draws on scaling-law thinking: overall loss can improve smoothly, but particular capabilities can appear discontinuously. A model may suddenly become reliable at something it failed before. If the team does not have evals, prototypes, and people using the model deeply, the jump can go unnoticed or unmanaged.

Token Maxing Means Experimentation, Not Waste

Lenny brings up the idea that people willing to spend heavily on tokens today are living in a future that will be cheaper later. Penn reframes it through a product lens: token spend is an input, but the real goal is experimentation. The best builders spend time with each research model because strategy is hard to form without touching fast-moving technology.

She also describes "working in public" internally. Anthropic teams tried early models in shared channels, watched each other experiment, copied useful ideas, and remixed them. The discovery process was communal, not purely individual. This matters because many AI use cases emerge when someone sees another person's attempt and realizes a nearby workflow is now possible.

Practical interpretation: do not measure only token budget. Measure how many meaningful experiments, reusable patterns, evals, and product insights the token budget produced.

Anthropic Labs: Discontinuous Bets

Penn describes Labs as the place for discontinuous bets that may not sit cleanly on the core roadmap. Labs pulls on threads such as Claude Code, Skills, Claude Design, MCP, and Claude Tag, asking whether there is a "there there" and what the 10x, 100x, or 1000x version could be.

The operating style is strongly held on theme, weakly held on exact prototype. Small pods or even one engineer can test a bold direction. Some ideas do not work yet and are paused until future model generations. Those prototypes still create value because they teach the organization what to revisit when the model catches up.

Small pods

Large ambiguous ideas can move faster with a small team that behaves like founders.

Strong theme

The team knows the direction, such as agentic coding or skills, even if the prototype changes weekly.

Future revisit

A rejected prototype may become viable one or two model generations later.

Research-product bridge

Labs sees around corners by pairing model capability with product intuition.

Timestamp Focus: 29:12 And The Product-Research Loop

The timestamp in your link lands in one of the most important parts of the conversation. Penn explains how her team works with researchers by turning user feedback into something that is understandable and actionable for model teams. This is a core pattern for any AI platform team.

A user might say "Claude hallucinated." That feedback is too vague to fix. The product team has to inspect the trajectory and ask what actually failed. Did Claude fail to call a tool? Did it search the right document but pull the wrong fact? Did it synthesize the right facts incorrectly? Was the issue alignment, overconfidence, context, retrieval, or tool access? The value of the PM is in moving from complaint to diagnosis to eval.

User feedback Diagnosis question Likely owner Potential eval
"Claude hallucinated." Was it missing knowledge, using the wrong source, or overconfident? Retrieval, model behavior, or alignment team. Fact-grounded answer set with source verification.
"It ignored my instruction." Which instruction failed, and was the format or priority ambiguous? Instruction-following and prompt/harness team. Instruction hierarchy and schema-following cases.
"It did not use the tool." Should the model have called a tool, and was the tool available? Tool-use, MCP, or agent runtime team. Tool-call decision benchmark.
"The result was wrong." Did reasoning, search, tool execution, or final synthesis fail? Diagnoser routes to the right sub-system. Trace-level failure classification.

Evals Are The New PRDs

Penn says her team has a saying: evals are the new PRDs. She does not mean PRDs are dead. She means that when the product is a model or agent behavior, the artifact that best defines the work may be the test set that captures the user's need.

The example is JSON schema following. Early Claude users complained that Claude was not good at following instructions. When Penn dug into the actual examples, a large share of the pain was that Claude was not producing the right JSON. The product move was to collect 30 to 40 concrete examples, turn them into an eval set, and add the eval to the model development loop. Over time, that failure became essentially solved.

  • 1Hear painCollect real user complaints or failed trajectories.
  • 2Inspect tokensRead prompts, responses, tool calls, retrieved facts, and failure context.
  • 3Cluster failureFind the repeated pattern behind vague feedback.
  • 4Write evalCreate examples, rubrics, expected outcomes, and pass conditions.
  • 5Improve loopRun it on future models or harnesses until the pain disappears.
    • For AI products, an eval can be a product requirement expressed as a reproducible failure.

    PRDs Still Matter, But Their Job Changes

    Penn is clear that Anthropic still uses PRDs. They are useful when a broad group needs alignment around goals, source-of-truth context, launch setup, legal, safety, engineering, and product surface coordination. Every model can still have a PRD-like alignment artifact.

    The distinction is that PRDs are not always the shortest path to product improvement. For a defined model failure, the eval is often more actionable. For an ambiguous new product direction such as computer use, the vision and alignment parts of a PRD remain valuable because the user pain and product shape may not yet be fully known.

    Use an eval when Use a PRD when
    The issue is a repeatable model, tool, or harness failure. The team needs shared context across many functions.
    You can express the desired behavior as examples, rubrics, or tests. The opportunity is ambiguous and needs narrative framing.
    The main stakeholder is research, model training, or agent runtime. The main stakeholders include product surface, legal, safety, GTM, and operations.
    The team needs to measure improvement over time. The team needs to align on what should exist at all.

    What Product People Need Now

    Penn says Anthropic's PM hiring loop has not fundamentally changed, but the meaning of the traits has become sharper. First-principles thinking matters more than pattern matching from old consumer or B2B SaaS playbooks. The question is not "what artifact do PMs usually produce?" The question is "what activity actually creates user value for this model, this user group, and this technology?"

    She also emphasizes hands-on work. Product leaders cannot only manage from a distance. They need to build, ship, read feedback, talk to users, and work directly with models. She says managers should spend a portion of time walking in the shoes of their teams so they can maintain a current theory of mind about where the model is improving and where it is still jagged.

    First principles

    Do what creates user value, not what old product rituals prescribe.

    Hands-on shipping

    Build with the technology yourself so judgment is grounded in real friction.

    Token-level taste

    Inspect trajectories, context, tools, and outputs deeply enough to diagnose failures.

    Ambition

    Ask what changes when Claude 8 exists, then build today's path to be forward compatible.

    Safeguards And Fallback UX

    As frontier models become more capable, pre-release testing, red teaming, and safeguards must evolve. Penn avoids policy specifics, but she names the product problem: safety systems should improve without destroying the user experience.

    She describes building fallback systems so users can still receive a useful response from another model or path when newer model access is constrained by safeguards. This matters for platform design because safety is not only a backend policy. It is a product experience. A blocked path should have an explainable, useful, and lower-risk alternative.

    Platform lesson: every high-risk agent capability needs a fallback UX. "No" is not enough; the user needs a safe next-best path.

    AI As A Thinking Partner, Not A Flatterer

    Penn talks about using Claude to prepare for difficult management conversations and as a sparring partner for thinking. The important point is that she does not want Claude to take over her point of view. She often forms her own view first, then uses Claude to refine, challenge, or deepen it.

    This connects to Anthropic's alignment work. A useful assistant should not only agree. It should know when to push back, when to propose a better idea, and when to help the user reach a stronger conclusion. Penn frames proactivity as not just doing scheduled tasks, but knowing when a new idea or objection would help.

    Bad AI behavior Better AI behavior Design implication
    Agrees with every user idea. Pushes back when assumptions are weak. Build critique modes and evidence checks into agents.
    Writes in generic AI voice. Preserves user intent, tone, and ownership. Separate drafting from verification and sign-off.
    Executes isolated tasks only. Knows when to suggest a better path. Define proactivity rules, not just task lists.
    Optimizes for compliance. Optimizes for better judgment. Measure whether the human came away with a stronger decision.

    Writing, Voice, And Verification

    Lenny asks why AI writing still often feels recognizable. Penn says writing and tone are active improvement areas, and that models have jagged edges: when agentic behavior improves, other rough edges such as writing style become more visible.

    Her most useful distinction is that the real issue may be less "who wrote this?" and more "who verified or signed off on this?" For some artifacts, such as a standard monthly business review, it may be fine for Claude to write much of it while a human reviews. For opinionated writing or personal judgment, the human should preserve the core point of view.

    Where Human Brains Still Matter

    Penn names judgment as the scarce human skill. Models can build many things, but product leaders still need to decide which things should be built, which direction matters, and how to interpret nuance. Judgment is accumulated through experience, context, and hard-earned taste.

    She also emphasizes persistence, point of view, curiosity, and an inner voice. Her parenting answer mirrors her product answer: people need their own perspective before they let AI reshape it. The more capable the model becomes, the more important it is that the human knows what they believe, what they are trying to accomplish, and what they will verify.

    Culture: Radical Ownership Plus Team Collaboration

    Penn says Anthropic shipped more model volume in one quarter of 2026 than in all of 2024. Sustaining that pace is not an individual sport. The culture depends on radical ownership, team collaboration, low ego, and people helping each other when launches are intense.

    She describes teammates staying up before launches even when they are not the directly responsible individual, helping review blog posts, improve demos, and be extra hands. This is part of what people describe as Anthropic's "hive mind": shared context, shared standards, and the ability to back each other up without every detail being centrally controlled.

    Agent-era reminder: working with agents can make people lonelier. High-performing AI teams still need human collaboration, shared thinking, and low-ego backup.

    My Read

    This is a very strong video for your agentic AI platform because it gives the missing bridge between product management and agent engineering. The biggest practical takeaway is that feedback must become structured. A BA or user saying "the agent was wrong" is just the beginning. The platform should guide them to identify whether the issue was source of truth, missing tool access, wrong tool choice, bad synthesis, policy, prompt, memory, or workflow design.

    I would directly borrow the phrase "evals are the new PRDs," but implement it carefully. A PRD still helps align humans. An eval helps align agents, models, skills, tools, and future releases. For a BA-friendly platform, the best experience is probably a hybrid: the BA writes a business flow, the platform generates agent specs and candidate evals, then every real failure can be promoted into the regression set.

    The second big takeaway is that vertical AI products need their own harness. Claude Code worked because it was not just a better model. It was a product surface with context, tools, loops, and user workflows. Your platform should treat each domain workflow the same way: not "one universal agent," but a vertical harness with source-of-truth data, tools, skills, evals, traces, fallback UX, and human ownership.

    Bottom line

    This interview says the future product manager is part user researcher, part eval author, part trace analyst, part systems designer, and part frontier explorer. That is exactly the builder profile your agentic AI platform should support.
