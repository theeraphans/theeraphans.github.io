Core Thesis

The durable layer is not the model. It is the company's contextualized operating memory.

Devin Kearns frames ontologies, world models, company brains, second brains, and LLM wikis as different names for a shared category: centralized company data enriched with structure, context, and relationships so agents can use it for real work. He sees this as more than a temporary AI trend. It is infrastructure for how companies will operate.

His argument starts from a practical observation. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Cursor, custom harnesses, and even the underlying models are increasingly interchangeable at the application layer. The interface may improve, the model may change, and the harness may be rebuilt. What cannot be swapped easily is the actual business: its history, jargon, exceptions, relationships, decision logic, customer nuance, and evolving workflows.

The strategic point is simple: the AI tool is useful, but the company-specific context that lets it act precisely is the defensible asset.
Terminology Map

The terms overlap, but each one emphasizes a slightly different part of the same system.

The video treats the terminology wave as noisy but directionally correct. The important thing is not whether a team says ontology, company brain, world model, AI memory layer, or LLM wiki. The important thing is whether the system can preserve business meaning and make it usable by humans and agents.

Term What it emphasizes How to think about it
Ontology Structured entities, relationships, events, rules, and valid actions. The nouns and verbs of the business, plus the edges that explain how work actually moves.
World model The system's overall understanding of the business environment. A digital twin of operations: what matters, what changed, what context is relevant, and what actions make sense.
Company brain Searchable company knowledge, SOPs, history, people, documents, workflows, and decisions. A more user-friendly framing, especially for smaller teams that want practical knowledge retrieval.
LLM wiki File-based, agent-readable knowledge that is easier for LLMs to retrieve and maintain. A lightweight memory surface made of structured markdown, indexes, links, and concept pages.
Ontology Anatomy

An ontology turns scattered artifacts into a web of business meaning.

Kearns explains ontology with ordinary business objects. A customer connects to deals. Deals connect to projects. Projects connect to tasks. Tasks connect to people. People connect to meetings. Meetings produce decisions. Decisions create follow-ups. The business is a changing graph, not a folder of isolated documents.

This matters because agents need context across time and across systems. If a single account moves from sales into delivery, the relevant context may live in CRM records, call transcripts, emails, contracts, project tasks, support tickets, meeting notes, and informal Slack messages. A long document can hold a narrative for one client, but it does not generalize well across accounts, roles, projects, or workflows.

Entity Customer

Account history, stakeholders, sensitivity, preferences, and active relationship state.

Transaction Deal

Sales stage, commercial terms, blockers, decision makers, and handoff requirements.

Delivery Project

Scope, tasks, owners, deadlines, dependencies, risks, and deliverables.

Interaction Meeting

Participants, decisions, commitments, unresolved questions, and follow-up actions.

Action Follow-up

Next step, owner, timing, policy constraints, confidence, and completion evidence.

The Moat

The business value lives in relationship context, not just in data access.

The talk repeatedly separates raw data from operating context. A company may have emails, Slack messages, CRM updates, sales calls, transcripts, and support tickets. But the valuable part is knowing which version is current, which source matters for a task, how people interpret signals, and what normally happens after a certain event.

This is why the speaker pushes back on the idea that buying AI seats and teaching everyone to use them is enough. Training employees on Claude or ChatGPT can help, but it does not build the future-proof layer. A durable AI strategy should ask how the company owns, updates, and operationalizes its context before it worries too much about the specific chat interface on top.

Raw data

What happened

Messages, documents, tickets, call transcripts, CRM fields, contracts, tasks, and event logs.

Context

Why it matters

Relationships, priority, recency, source trust, policy constraints, customer nuance, and informal norms.

Action logic

What to do next

Valid actions, escalation rules, confidence thresholds, human approvals, and write-back behavior.

Data Sharing Risk

Giving a frontier tool your context can mean giving away the operating logic of the business.

Kearns' strongest warning is that companies are often casual about sending high-value context into frontier-model products. The sensitive part is not only customer records or confidential documents. It is the accumulated operating knowledge: internal shorthand, unspoken communication norms, who makes which decision, why a client needs a different approach, and which exceptions are understood but not written down.

The speaker's position is not anti-model-provider. It is pro-ownership. Companies can still use powerful external models, but they should keep the contextualized business layer under their own control and expose only what a task requires. The future question is less "Which model did we choose?" and more "What company context can the model access, under what rules, and is that context sufficient for safe action?"

Context type Why it is valuable Platform control needed
Customer nuance Shows how to communicate, escalate, negotiate, and avoid relationship damage. Account-level permissions, source citations, and task-scoped disclosure.
Tribal knowledge Captures habits and rules that make operations work but rarely appear in SOPs. Curated memory, review loops, provenance, and policy tagging.
Decision patterns Explains how the company actually chooses between tradeoffs. Approval boundaries, confidence scoring, and audit trails.
Process exceptions Prevents agents from applying a generic process where the business uses judgment. Exception registry, escalation rules, and abstention policies.
Context Freshness

Static context engineering decays as the business changes.

The video argues that many agent workflows work for a while and then lose relevance. The company changes in small ways: people move roles, goals shift, process details change, customers behave differently, and the old retrieval logic no longer gathers the right context. The system did not necessarily fail because the model got worse. It failed because the context layer went stale.

This is why Kearns likes the idea of a living world model. If the system captures events and updates entities, relationships, workflows, success criteria, and safe actions over time, the harness can keep working even as models are swapped or simplified. The ideal direction is a self-maintaining context layer, not a pile of one-off prompt and retrieval scripts.

Brittle pattern

One-off retrieval logic

An email agent, CRM agent, scheduling agent, and document agent each rebuild context differently for each use case.

Compounding pattern

Shared contextual layer

Agents query the same structured source of truth and use event updates to keep relationships, rules, and context current.

Event-Driven Architecture

The practical system starts by capturing events and turning them into reusable context.

Kearns describes an event-driven direction for real agentic work. An email comes in. The system captures the event at the source, identifies who is involved, determines which account or project it belongs to, decides whether a task should be created, stores the structured event, and makes the resulting context retrievable for humans and agents.

The storage layer is flexible. It could be a knowledge graph, relational database, vector database, or hybrid architecture. The important part is the semantic contract: events become organized into the company world model, connected to entities and workflows, and evaluated against success criteria and policy boundaries.

1 Capture event

Email, ticket, CRM update, meeting, contract change, or support issue arrives.

2 Contextualize

Identify people, account, project, intent, history, policy, and likely next action.

3 Store structure

Write entities, edges, event facts, sources, timestamps, and confidence into memory.

4 Retrieve for work

Agents and humans get pre-contextualized data instead of searching scattered systems.

5 Act or abstain

Execute, ask for approval, spawn sub-agents, or refuse to act when risk is too high.

Safety Layer

A real company world model must know when not to act.

The video makes an important distinction: a company brain should not be only a retrieval engine. If the agent lacks confidence, the requested action is risky, or the policy context is incomplete, the system should abstain. That means "do nothing yet" is a valid output, not a failure.

This is a useful design point for agentic platforms. Confidence scoring is not enough by itself. The platform needs policies, risk tiers, source quality, permission boundaries, approval paths, and audit evidence. A world model should tell the agent not only what is true, but what action is allowed and what uncertainty should block automation.

Question Agent should proceed when Agent should abstain when
Is the context current? Sources are fresh, relevant, and tied to the correct entity. The latest state is missing, conflicting, or older than the workflow allows.
Is the action permitted? The user's role, workflow policy, and account permissions allow the action. The action crosses a policy, money, legal, security, or customer-risk boundary.
Is confidence sufficient? The system can cite evidence and the success criteria are clear. The agent would need to guess, invent context, or rely on weak signals.
Is human judgment required? The work is routine, reversible, and covered by SOP or prior examples. The decision is ambiguous, high-impact, political, or exception-heavy.
Platform Takeaways

For an agentic AI platform, the ontology layer is the product foundation.

This video maps closely to the platform problem you have been describing: how business analysts can define SOPs, agent personas, skills, tools, and flows without forcing engineers to hard-code every workflow. The answer implied by the talk is to separate the company memory layer from the agent execution layer.

Business users should be able to model the domain in a controlled language: entity types, relationships, events, SOP states, permissions, valid tools, confidence rules, and escalation policies. Agents should then query that layer as their operating context, rather than each agent carrying a giant bespoke prompt.

  • Build entity templates: account, customer, policy, claim, order, project, asset, ticket, document, meeting, decision, task, and follow-up.
  • Model events explicitly: each workflow should start from an event type with source, payload, actor, affected entities, and expected outcome.
  • Separate cognitive and actionable skills: cognitive skills read, classify, reason, compare, and summarize; actionable skills require tools, permission checks, and write-back rules.
  • Make SOPs graph-aware: every SOP step should declare required context, evidence sources, allowed tools, exit criteria, and abstention rules.
  • Keep personas compact: agent prompts should reference role, responsibility, decision boundary, and memory contracts instead of stuffing all business knowledge into the prompt.
  • Track context quality: freshness, source trust, conflicts, missing fields, and human overrides should become first-class runtime signals.
Flow Markdown

A reusable business-flow template inspired by the talk.

Use this as a starting format for turning business flow into agent-readable markdown. The goal is to let BA-side users define a process while still giving engineering enough structure for tools, permissions, memory, evaluation, and tracing. 

---
flow_id: inbound_customer_email_to_follow_up
domain: customer_operations
trigger_event:
  type: email.received
  source: gmail_or_outlook
  required_fields: [sender, recipients, subject, body, received_at]
entities:
  - customer_account
  - contact
  - active_project
  - open_ticket
  - prior_decision
memory_contract:
  retrieve:
    - latest_account_summary
    - unresolved_tasks
    - prior_meetings
    - similar_support_cases
    - communication_preferences
  freshness_policy:
    account_summary_max_age_days: 14
    ticket_state_must_be_current: true
agent_roles:
  master_agent:
    responsibility: route_event_and_select_workflow
    may_spawn: [context_research_agent, draft_response_agent, policy_check_agent]
  context_research_agent:
    skill_type: cognitive
    responsibility: gather_and_compare_relevant_context
  draft_response_agent:
    skill_type: actionable
    required_tools: [email_draft_tool]
    responsibility: produce_response_draft_only
  policy_check_agent:
    skill_type: cognitive
    responsibility: verify_permission_risk_and_abstention
steps:
  - id: classify_event
    required_context: [sender, account_match, intent]
    output: event_classification
  - id: retrieve_context
    required_context: [account_id, active_project_id]
    output: evidence_pack
  - id: decide_action
    policy:
      abstain_if:
        - account_match_confidence_below: 0.85
        - customer_risk_tier_in: [high, regulated]
        - unresolved_conflicting_context: true
    output: action_plan
  - id: draft_or_escalate
    allowed_tools: [email_draft_tool, task_create_tool]
    human_approval_required_if:
      - pricing_or_contract_terms_present: true
      - legal_or_security_topic_present: true
success_metrics:
  - correct_account_link_rate
  - human_edit_distance_on_drafts
  - unnecessary_escalation_rate
  - unsafe_action_attempts
  - customer_response_time
trace_requirements:
  - cite_sources_used
  - log_retrieved_entities
  - log_tool_calls
  - record_abstention_reason
---
