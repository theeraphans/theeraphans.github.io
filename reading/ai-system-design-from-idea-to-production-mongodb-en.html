AI System Design From Idea to Production

A practical summary of Apoorva Joshi's framework for turning an AI idea into a production system: define requirements first, design the simplest architecture that fits the constraints, evaluate before and after launch, then optimize for accuracy, cost, latency, and reliability.

Video: AI System Design: From Idea to Production Speaker: Apoorva Joshi, MongoDB Transcript: 4,200 words

The Short Version

The talk argues that AI coding and agents do not remove the need for product requirements or system design. They make the specification more important. For real products, you need to define the business problem, constraints, AI autonomy level, data strategy, architecture pattern, evaluation metrics, guardrails, feedback loop, and optimization plan before shipping.

  • Start with the user problem and constraints, not with "should this be an agent?"
  • Design the simplest system, evaluate it, then add complexity only where measured gaps appear.
  • Production readiness means monitoring quality, guardrails, cost, latency, reliability, and human overrides.

Framework

The talk gives a repeatable four-phase path from idea to production

Apoorva's core message is that "just ship it" is dangerous once an AI system affects real users, regulated data, or business decisions. The hard part shifts from writing code to defining the specification, the architecture, and the evaluation criteria that let AI builders know they are building the right thing.

1. Product requirements Define the user, business pain, constraints, AI role, autonomy, and success metrics.
2. System design Choose data sources, retrieval strategy, workflow pattern, human review, and stack.
3. Evaluation and monitoring Measure guardrails, quality, domain outcomes, system health, and production regressions.
4. Optimization Improve accuracy, cost, latency, and reliability before and after launch.

The product spec is now the hard part. AI can help build faster, but it cannot guess your constraints for you.

Product Requirements

The business problem should be measurable and solution-agnostic

The example system supports medical reviewers who process health insurance claims. The business problem is not "build an agent." It is that reviewers spend too long cross-referencing clinical guidelines, coverage policies, and patient history before reaching a decision.

Problem

Describe the current pain precisely

State who the user is, what the current process looks like, and how much pain or delay exists today.

Constraints

Collect business rules before design

Patient data location, approved model vendors, compliance, procurement, human-review rules, and SLAs shape the architecture.

Success

Turn the goal into a metric

The sample metric reduces urgent claim review time from days to one hour within a defined launch window.

Requirement Area Decision From The Talk Why It Matters
AI role Complementary, because claim reviews already happen without AI. The AI should improve speed and reviewer experience, not pretend to replace the entire business process.
Trigger Reactive, triggered when a claim application is submitted. This supports a controlled workflow instead of a proactive autonomous agent.
Autonomy Semi-autonomous, because denials and complex cases require human review. The architecture must include escalation, explanation, and final human decision points.
Data Strategy

Retrieval quality starts with data readiness, update cadence, and access format

The claims review system needs clinical guidelines, coverage policies, and patient claims history. Each source has a different format, update frequency, and retrieval technique. The system design should follow those facts instead of assuming one generic vector search solves everything.

Clinical guidelines

Long documents need processing

Chunk, embed, and attach metadata such as procedure names and publication dates for retrieval.

Coverage policies

Policy text needs hybrid retrieval

Use vector search with metadata filters or keyword search because medical and billing codes may be exact-match signals.

Claims history

Structured records need exact lookup

Patient claim history can come from MongoDB by patient identifier, with sensitive data removed before LLM use.

Update cadence is a product requirement. Annual guidelines, quarterly policies, and frequently changing claims history need different pipelines, or the AI may make decisions from stale context.

System Design

The recommended architecture is not a free-roaming agent. It is RAG plus controlled workflow.

The talk maps the claim journey before choosing AI patterns. The system receives a claim and clinical notes, retrieves guidelines, policies, and claim history, asks the LLM for a cited recommendation, escalates complex or denial cases to humans, and logs the final decision with reasoning.

Claim input Claim request and physician notes enter the system from the application.
Retrieval Guidelines, policies, and patient history are gathered through hybrid and exact-match retrieval.
LLM assessment The model generates an approval or denial recommendation with citations.
Human review Complex cases and denial decisions are escalated to qualified reviewers.
Decision log The final verdict, reasoning, and feedback are stored back in MongoDB.
  • RAG is needed. The model needs external clinical and policy context to make a grounded recommendation.
  • Control flow fits better than full autonomy. The sequence is known and high stakes, so code or workflow design should determine the steps.
  • LLM-as-router may apply. A model can classify whether a case is complex if the business rules are not already deterministic.
  • Human-in-the-loop is mandatory. Business constraints require senior physician or medical reviewer involvement in certain cases.
UX and Feedback

The product interface should capture reviewer trust, overrides, and bad citations

The system's output is not only a verdict. It must explain itself. For this use case, explanation means citations from clinical guidelines and coverage policies. Reviewers also need feedback channels, such as overriding the AI verdict and flagging irrelevant citations.

User experience

Make the recommendation reviewable

Show approval or denial, evidence, cited policy text, patient-history signals, and escalation reason in the existing reviewer workflow.

Feedback loop

Turn human corrections into monitoring signals

Capture verdict overrides, override reasons, flagged citations, and review time so the system can improve after launch.

Evaluation and Monitoring

Measure guardrails, quality, domain outcomes, and system health before and after launch

Evaluation happens before shipping. Monitoring happens after shipping. The talk stresses that both matter because LLM systems are probabilistic and can produce invalid, irrelevant, hallucinated, or harmful outputs unless the acceptable boundaries are defined and measured.

Metric Type Claims Review Metric What It Reveals
Input guardrail Claim rejection rate Whether the system is rejecting invalid or irrelevant inputs too often or too rarely.
Output guardrail Missing citation rate Whether recommendations are failing the evidence requirement.
Response quality Faithfulness Whether approval or denial reasoning is grounded in retrieved guidelines and policies.
Domain outcome Claim processing time Whether the system is solving the actual business problem.
System health Cost per recommendation Whether the system can operate within budget at production volume.
Production signal Human override rate and review duration Whether reviewers trust the output and can understand it quickly.

The practical takeaway is simple: if your platform does not ask for metrics while the flow is being designed, it will be hard to prove whether the AI system works later.

Optimization

Accuracy is only one production constraint

Once the prototype appears accurate, production still requires cost, latency, and reliability work. The talk organizes optimization into techniques that improve context quality, reduce expensive work, and make output format dependable.

Accuracy

Improve what reaches the context window

Use prompt engineering, retrieval improvements, reranking, and memory where they fit the product.

Cost and latency

Avoid unnecessary expensive calls

Semantic caching can help with similar historical claims, while batch processing can reduce per-claim overhead.

Reliability

Make outputs predictable

Structured outputs are especially important because the decision and citations must appear in a machine-checkable format.

Platform Takeaways

What this means for a BA-buildable agentic AI platform

This talk is valuable because it gives the missing product-design layer before Master Agent, Sub-Agent, Skill, and Tool MCP design. A BA-facing platform should guide users through requirements and evaluation before letting them assemble agents.

  • Make Product Requirements a required artifact. Flow Markdown should start with user, current state, pain metric, constraints, AI role, autonomy level, and success metric.
  • Do not ask "agent or multi-agent" too early. First map the business flow, data, risk, and human decision points.
  • Compile requirements into architecture hints. Semi-autonomous plus human-required denial review should recommend controlled workflow and human-in-loop patterns.
  • Treat data cadence as a runtime requirement. BA users should declare how often each source changes, so pipelines and retrieval freshness can be planned.
  • Make citations a contract, not a nice-to-have. For regulated workflows, evidence fields should be required in the output schema.
  • Attach metrics to every flow before implementation. Guardrail compliance, faithfulness, task success, cost, latency, and human override signals should be defined up front.
  • Separate cognitive and actionable skills by risk. Information synthesis can be cognitive; approval, denial, update, or notification steps need human review, audit logs, and tool permissions.
  • Use evaluation gaps to justify complexity. Add routers, agents, fine-tuning, reranking, or semantic caching only after measurement shows the simpler design is failing.
  • Expose post-launch feedback loops. Human overrides and flagged evidence should become monitoring signals and future eval cases.
