The State of AI in 2026: Why Productivity Has Not Yet Become ROI

AI adoption and individual benefits are rising quickly. Enterprise financial impact is not. McKinsey's survey suggests the missing bridge is workflow and organizational redesign.

McKinsey & Company31-page reportAugust 2026Summary run September 9, 2026 · 09:00 ICT

This article summarizes the supplied report and redraws selected comparisons for mobile reading. All percentages are self-reported survey responses, not audited operational or financial measurements.

Executive summary

AI conviction is growing faster than attributable returns

McKinsey's 2026 survey finds that organizations are moving from experiments toward enterprise deployment. Eighty-nine percent report regular AI use in at least one business function, and 44 percent say AI has reached at least the scaling phase across the enterprise, up six percentage points from 2025. Large companies are widening their lead in agentic AI.

At the same time, the percentage attributing any positive EBIT impact to AI remains roughly flat at 37 percent. Only about 6 percent meet McKinsey's stricter definition of an AI high performer: at least 5 percent of EBIT attributed to AI plus “significant” reported value. The central tension is therefore not adoption versus rejection. It is individual usefulness versus organizational value capture.

The report's main argument

Organizations that transform workflows, operating models, leadership behavior, measurement, and workforce planning are more likely to convert AI capability into financial value than organizations that merely distribute tools.

Adoption and scale

Large enterprises are pulling ahead in agentic AI

Among organizations with at least $1 billion in annual revenue, 54 percent report scaling AI across the enterprise, compared with roughly one-third of smaller organizations. The gap is especially visible for agents: 40 percent of respondents at large enterprises report scaling agents in at least one function, up from 27 percent in 2025. Smaller organizations remained nearly flat, moving from 21 to 22 percent.

Scale and breadth are also increasing: the share using AI in three or more functions rose from 51 to 56 percent. Chatbots are most mature, with 47 percent reporting enterprise scaling. About two in ten report scaling software coding agents, rising to 31 percent among larger companies.

The likely explanation is not simply stronger models. Large companies can finance shared platforms, security, integration, change management, governance, and process redesign. Smaller firms may adopt tools quickly at the individual level but lack the fixed investment needed to standardize agents across functions.

Coding agents

Build-versus-buy decisions are beginning to move

Thirty-two percent of respondents say their organization decided against buying at least one software product or feature because agentic coding tools made internal development possible. The pattern is strongest in technology and healthcare, followed by professional services and energy and materials.

This is a strategic budget shift, not proof that packaged software is disappearing. Coding agents reduce implementation cost, but internal ownership creates maintenance, security, compliance, integration, and support obligations. The rational decision changes when the requirement is differentiating, tightly integrated, or poorly served by generic software. Commodity functions with mature vendors may still be cheaper to buy.

Productivity–ROI gap

Personal acceleration does not automatically improve enterprise economics

Among respondents who use AI in their roles, 80 percent say it improves productivity and about half say it helps them make better decisions. Benefits are broadly consistent across organizational levels, although individual contributors report somewhat lower gains than leaders on several measures.

These are meaningful signals, but saved time is not the same as captured value. An employee can produce a document faster while the downstream approval queue remains unchanged. A developer can generate more code while testing, integration, security review, or product adoption becomes the bottleneck. If capacity is neither redeployed nor translated into revenue, margin, quality, or cycle-time improvement, the enterprise P&L may not change.

The gap can also reflect measurement lag. Enterprise transformation takes longer than tool adoption, and attributing EBIT across many interacting initiatives is difficult. The flat 37 percent should therefore be read as a warning about value capture and measurement, not a clean estimate of AI's causal return.

Cost and investment

Cheaper tokens can still produce a larger bill

One in five respondents says AI-related operating cost, including token cost, constrained use. The report describes “tokenomics” becoming a CFO and chief AI officer concern: unit prices decline while agentic software development and complex reasoning consume more tokens, make more tool calls, and run for longer.

Yet 60 percent expect enterprise AI investment to increase over the next year. This is not necessarily contradictory. A technology can be valuable enough to fund while still requiring active cost engineering. The relevant denominator is business value, not tokens alone.

  1. Measure cost per completed, accepted business outcome rather than cost per request.
  2. Separate model inference from tools, retrieval, storage, human review, retries, and failure recovery.
  3. Route tasks by required capability instead of sending every step to the most expensive model.
  4. Set budgets and stop conditions for autonomous loops.
  5. Cache stable context and reuse verified artifacts rather than regenerating them.
  6. Track rework: inexpensive output that creates downstream correction can have negative ROI.

High performers

The differentiator is coherent transformation, not one magic practice

AI high performers account for about 6 percent of respondents. In the report's comparison, High performers (n = 92) are contrasted with all other respondents (n = 1,429). Both groups emphasize efficiency: 78 percent of high performers and 82 percent of others. The stronger separation appears in growth and innovation goals.

Nearly three-quarters of high performers report fundamentally redesigning workflows, up from 55 percent the prior year, versus roughly one-quarter of others. They are also 3.3 times more likely to expect AI-driven enterprise transformation within three years.

The broader practice bundle includes senior-leader ownership, defined impact measurement, strategic workforce planning, explicit human-in-the-loop decisions, cost management, a transformation office with authority, semantic data infrastructure, iterative development, larger budget commitment, and more active risk mitigation.

Important interpretation

This is correlation inside a self-reported survey. High performers may adopt these practices because they already possess stronger leadership, data, talent, budgets, and operating discipline. The report identifies a plausible pattern, not a randomized causal recipe.

Workforce

Expected reductions repeatedly exceed realized reductions

In the 2025 survey, 32 percent expected AI-related enterprise workforce decline over the following year. In 2026, only 14 percent report that such a decline actually occurred, while 66 percent report little or no change. The new forecast has nevertheless risen: 39 percent now expect a decline during the next year, and 43 percent expect little or no change.

McKinsey checked the year-over-year finding against the 552 people who completed both surveys and says the pattern remained consistent. Even so, expectation data measure sentiment and planning, not future outcomes. Organizations may reduce hiring, redeploy work, increase output, or change skill composition without immediately reducing total head count.

The survey also finds most respondents do not see AI as damaging their own career prospects. This creates another gap: people may forecast aggregate job loss while remaining more optimistic about their personal adaptability.

Research method

What the survey can and cannot establish

FieldworkMay 4 to June 8, 2026

The online survey received responses from 1,719 participants in 97 nations.

WeightingNational GDP contribution

Responses were weighted to adjust for country-level response-rate differences.

Company size36% from $1B+ organizations

Large-enterprise comparisons use 595 respondents; smaller-company comparisons use 1,035 in cited exhibits.

PerspectiveParticipants at multiple levels

The survey spans regions, industries, company sizes, functions, and tenures.

StrengthBroad directional comparison

Repeated questions and a longitudinal respondent subset help identify changing perceptions.

LimitSelf-reported organizational outcomes

Respondents may not know enterprise deployment, cost, or EBIT attribution with equal accuracy.

Percentages often use different denominators. Individual productivity questions were asked only of people using AI in their roles. Agent questions may be rebased to the full sample. Function-level cost and revenue questions apply only where AI is used in that function. High-performer analysis uses a small selected group. These conditions matter when comparing charts.

The survey is cross-sectional and observational. It cannot prove that workflow redesign causes EBIT improvement, that AI caused a reported head-count change, or that a stated decision not to buy software produced lower total cost. It is best treated as a map of reported adoption, beliefs, and associated practices.

Operating implications

What leaders should do differently

  1. Choose business outcomes and accountable owners before selecting agent technology.
  2. Map the entire workflow, including waits, approvals, exceptions, and downstream verification.
  3. Redesign roles and decision rights instead of placing AI inside every existing step.
  4. Measure baseline cost, cycle time, quality, risk, revenue, and capacity before deployment.
  5. Track individual productivity separately from captured organizational value.
  6. Give transformation teams authority to remove process bottlenecks across functions.
  7. Make token, tool, retry, storage, and human-review cost visible per accepted outcome.
  8. Use coding agents to revisit build-versus-buy decisions, while pricing long-term ownership honestly.
  9. Define when human validation is required and enforce it at consequential action boundaries.
  10. Revisit workforce plans with observed task changes rather than headline employment forecasts.

Critical assessment

The report is strongest as an organizational warning

The headline numbers coherently describe a transition: AI is normalizing as workplace infrastructure, agents are entering scale unevenly, costs are becoming material, and financial capture lags personal enthusiasm. The report is especially useful in resisting the assumption that distributing copilots equals transformation.

Its high-performer section is suggestive but should not become a checklist marketed as causation. The group is small, selected using self-reported financial attribution, and likely differs from peers in many preexisting ways. Likewise, “decided against buying software” does not demonstrate that internal building was cheaper or successful.

The most defensible conclusion is narrower and more useful: as AI output increases, the limiting factors migrate into workflow design, integration, evaluation, leadership, measurement, and change management. Organizations must engineer those systems if they want local productivity to appear in enterprise economics.

Takeaways

Twelve ideas worth keeping

  1. AI use is nearly ubiquitous, but enterprise scaling is still far from universal.
  2. Large enterprises are extending their lead in agent deployment.
  3. Coding agents are already changing some build-versus-buy decisions.
  4. Individual productivity and enterprise EBIT measure different layers of value.
  5. Saved time has no financial effect until capacity or outcomes change.
  6. AI operating costs constrain one in five respondents despite falling unit prices.
  7. Most organizations still plan to increase investment.
  8. High performers pursue growth and innovation in addition to efficiency.
  9. Workflow redesign is much more common among reported high performers.
  10. Past workforce reductions were much smaller than respondents had predicted.
  11. Survey associations are not proof of causal impact.
  12. The road to ROI runs through operating-model change, not tool adoption alone.
