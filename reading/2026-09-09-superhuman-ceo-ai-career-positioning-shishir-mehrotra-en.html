The AI Career Ladder Is Moving Up

Superhuman CEO Shishir Mehrotra argues that AI is compressing execution and raising the value of problem selection, questions, judgment, and influence. The durable advantage is learning how to direct abundant capability.

Executive summary

AI changes what people are promoted for

Shishir Mehrotra's central argument is that the job someone performs today and the capability required for their next promotion are increasingly different. Early-career workers have traditionally proved themselves by executing larger tasks. Senior contributors create value by identifying the right problem, choosing among possible solutions, coordinating people, and ensuring that work reaches the market. As AI absorbs more execution, everyone is pushed toward those higher-order responsibilities sooner.

He does not treat this as permission to skip practice. Judgment is itself a learned skill, and it develops through repeated decisions followed by fast feedback. His practical advice is to build projects, write publicly, and rehearse difficult forms of thinking in low-stakes environments. This simultaneously creates evidence of ability and helps a candidate avoid the undifferentiated recruiting funnel.

The second half demonstrates Superhuman Go, an agent layer derived from Grammarly's ability to understand what a person is writing across applications. Mehrotra distinguishes three AI interaction models: Chat waits for a question, Do accepts delegated tasks, and Assist appears inside the current workflow before the user remembers to ask. The product thesis is that contextual, ambient assistance can generate thousands of micro-interventions where a chat tool may receive only a handful of deliberate prompts.

The interview's most useful idea

When execution becomes cheap, value moves upstream to deciding what deserves execution and downstream to judging whether the result is good enough to matter.

Career positioning · 00:54

Stay out of the recruiting folder

Mehrotra says his most consequential hires and career transitions rarely began as formal recruiting conversations. A useful paper he wrote about product bundling reached Spotify CEO Daniel Ek through other people and eventually led to a long-running board role. The artifact created a reason to talk before anyone was asking for a job.

His advice is to become interesting in public: start projects, write ideas that can travel, and create work that prompts a conversation. AI lowers the cost of making artifacts and distribution channels make them easier to share, but this also increases supply. Generic output will not differentiate anyone; a distinct question, evidence, judgment, or point of view must remain visible.

Career framework · 06:40

Problem → Solution → How → Execution

P · ProblemWhat should we solve?

Identify the consequential need, constraint, or opportunity.

S · SolutionWhat is the best answer?

Generate options and apply taste to choose among them.

H · HowHow will it land?

Align people, test the market, and design the path to adoption.

E · ExecutionHow do we build it?

Produce, operate, and refine the actual output.

Early in a career, progression often means larger execution scope. In the middle, people encounter what Mehrotra calls a trough of disillusionment: two people may own similarly sized work, yet one advances because of how they frame and lead it. At higher levels, scope grows again, but now the person owns problem spaces rather than only assigned tasks.

AI raises the whole ladder. It is already powerful at execution, can propose solutions, and can help plan delivery. It can serve as a thought partner at every level, but the human still has to judge outputs and provide a productive frame. The scarce skill becomes knowing which problem to pursue and recognizing a strong result.

Automation · 10:20

“Replacement” hides the reconfiguration of work

Mehrotra dislikes framing every technology shift as a fixed number of jobs divided between humans and machines. His analogy is the power drill: it changed the valuable skill from turning a screwdriver quickly to building larger things. That analogy usefully highlights expansion, but it should not erase transition costs. Some roles do disappear, entry-level pathways can narrow, and workers do not automatically receive the time or support to retrain.

Marketing illustrates the repeated pattern. The industry moved from a small number of annual campaigns to millions of digital variations and hired more systems-oriented practitioners. Taste and message design did not vanish; they moved to a different point in the system. With generative AI, drafting gets cheaper while strategy, audience understanding, measurement, and judgment become more important.

Learning · 04:00

Build judgment through low-stakes practice

Creative and managerial judgment cannot be learned only during consequential meetings. That would be like learning guitar exclusively through recitals or basketball only in broadcast games. Mehrotra recommends side projects, games, practice with friends, and repeated exercises where being wrong is inexpensive and feedback arrives quickly.

ChoosePick a judgment skill.

Problem framing, product taste, interviewing, prioritization, or AI-centric design.

Lower stakesCreate a private practice arena.

Use a side project or simulation where experimentation will not damage live work.

RepeatRun many short cycles.

Make the decision, observe the outcome, and compare alternatives.

Publish evidenceTurn selected learning into an artifact.

A thoughtful project or essay can demonstrate judgment better than a claim on a resume.

Question quality · 15:25

The Eigenquestion answers the most other questions

Borrowing language from eigenvectors, Mehrotra defines an Eigenquestion as the most discriminating question in a set: answering it collapses the greatest amount of uncertainty and often resolves several downstream questions at once. He says this has become his strongest signal when evaluating people.

His interview exercise imagines scientists inventing teleportation. Candidates first generate many questions about safety, size, cost, speed, and mechanics. Then they may ask only two. The constraint reveals whether they can find questions that divide the possibility space: Is it safe enough for people? What would people use it for? The goal is not one canonical answer but the ability to select high-information questions.

A reusable test

Before asking “What should we do next?”, list the unknowns and ask: Which single answer would most change our decision or make the other questions unnecessary?

Product architecture · 22:20

Grammarly's distribution becomes an agent surface

Mehrotra describes Grammarly as two conceptual halves. The visible writing assistant identifies moments where help is useful. Beneath it is an “AI superhighway” that can read context and present suggestions across web and desktop applications. Superhuman Go extends that substrate from grammar assistance to user-configured agents with instructions, knowledge, connectors, MCP tools, and triggers.

A “while writing” trigger is the differentiator in the demonstration. A launch project manager can flag an inaccurate status while someone drafts an announcement. A knowledge checker can compare claims with connected sources. A placeholder filler can find quotes or facts when the writer leaves bracketed gaps. A scheduling assistant can check calendars and propose a valid time inside the message being composed.

Interaction model · 28:35

Chat → Do → Assist

ChatAsk for an answer

The user notices a need, opens an AI surface, supplies context, and prompts.

DoDelegate a task

The user adds work to an agent's queue and expects a completed outcome.

AssistIntervene in context

The agent observes the current workflow and offers help before being explicitly invoked.

Assist reduces the “remember to use AI” tax. A person may know that a fact should be checked or a calendar conflict verified but fail to interrupt their writing, open another tool, provide context, and ask. An ambient agent can make that micro-check routine.

The tradeoff is attention and trust. Proactive systems need high precision, visible evidence, easy dismissal, scoped data access, and controls for when they may interrupt. Thousands of low-quality interventions would create noise rather than leverage. The success metric should be accepted, correct help per interruption, not raw query volume.

Leadership · 34:10

Measure success through the success of others

The closing story concerns Silicon Valley coach Bill Campbell. When offered equity for helping Mehrotra's early company, Campbell declined and explained that he tracked how many people he had supported went on to become Fortune 500 CEOs. Mehrotra took from this a non-zero-sum model of management: a leader should actively root for people who eventually leave and succeed elsewhere.

This complements the AI argument. If tools increase every individual's execution capacity, management becomes less about assigning and monitoring tasks and more about sharpening questions, developing judgment, creating conditions for practice, and enabling other people to achieve larger outcomes.

Critical assessment

Strong career advice, with caveats around access and automation

StrongArtifacts create credible signals.

Visible work demonstrates initiative and judgment more concretely than self-description.

StrongLow-stakes practice makes tacit skills trainable.

Fast feedback and repetition are credible mechanisms for building judgment.

StrongEigenquestions improve decision efficiency.

Prioritizing high-information questions is useful across product, management, and research.

CaveatExecution remains part of judgment.

People often acquire taste by doing the work; removing junior execution can weaken the learning ladder.

CaveatNot everyone can self-fund practice.

Side projects require time, tools, stability, and access that are distributed unevenly.

Product riskAmbient assistance can become ambient distraction.

Superhuman Go's claims need evaluation for correctness, interruption cost, privacy, and real task outcomes.

The best reading is not “execution no longer matters.” It is that execution alone is becoming a weaker differentiator. Practitioners still need enough domain depth to detect errors, understand constraints, and know what excellent work looks like. AI raises the premium on judgment while making the apprenticeship that produces judgment more important to design deliberately.

Practical playbook

What to do over the next 12 months

  1. Choose one domain where you already know enough to judge quality.
  2. List the work as Problem, Solution, How, and Execution; identify where you currently contribute.
  3. Use AI to compress execution, then reinvest the saved time in problem discovery and evaluation.
  4. Create a low-stakes weekly exercise for a judgment skill you need next.
  5. For important decisions, write ten questions and select the one that removes the most uncertainty.
  6. Build one useful public artifact that reveals your thinking, not just AI-polished output.
  7. Observe repeated moments where you forget to ask AI for help; these are candidates for contextual assistance.
  8. Measure proactive agents by correct accepted suggestions, not activity volume.
  9. Keep practicing implementation deeply enough to audit AI-generated work.
  10. Help colleagues become more capable and count their progress as part of your own leadership output.

Takeaways

Ten ideas worth keeping

  1. AI shifts career value from executing assigned work toward selecting and framing worthwhile work.
  2. Judgment is learned through decisions and feedback, not acquired as a seniority badge.
  3. Practice creative and managerial skills where mistakes are cheap.
  4. Public artifacts can create opportunities before a formal job conversation begins.
  5. The best question is often the one that eliminates the most other questions.
  6. AI can assist every layer of work, but humans remain accountable for framing and evaluation.
  7. Execution is less differentiating, not unnecessary.
  8. Ambient assistance removes the need to remember to prompt, but must earn the right to interrupt.
  9. Agent usefulness depends on current context, reliable knowledge, tools, and carefully chosen triggers.
  10. A strong leader measures success partly through the people they help succeed.
