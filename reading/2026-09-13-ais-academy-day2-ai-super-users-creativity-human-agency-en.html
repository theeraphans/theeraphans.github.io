AIS Academy for THAIs 2026 · Day 2

AI Super Users, Creativity, and Human Agency

Moving beyond chat: a detailed reading guide to local AI capability, delegated work, creative judgment, relationships, and staying grounded as technology accelerates.

This is a thematic reading guide, not a verbatim transcript or an exhaustive account of every demonstration. The automatic captions contain errors, especially in names, model labels, and numbers.

Executive summary

More capability does not remove human responsibility

The program moves across three levels of AI adoption. At the national level, it asks how Thailand can participate in creating value rather than remain only a buyer of foreign technology. At the working level, it encourages people to move beyond asking questions and start building applications or delegating multi-step tasks. At the personal level, it asks what happens when AI becomes a creative partner, an always-available companion, or a source of advice.

The most useful thread is not that AI makes all expertise obsolete. It is that cheaper execution changes where human effort matters. Defining the problem, connecting local knowledge, choosing a story, checking an output, setting a boundary, and accepting consequences remain central.

Build capability

Use local knowledge, infrastructure, institutions, and practical experimentation.

Exercise judgment

Direct the work, evaluate quality, and distinguish a demonstration from dependable delivery.

Keep agency

Choose your goals, retain responsibility, and stay connected to life outside the interface.

This three-part map is editorial synthesis. It is not a named framework presented by the event.

Opening · Approximately 00:00–00:06

Why an academy should reach beyond its own employees

The opening address explains AIS Academy as an initiative that began with developing people inside the organization and then extended knowledge outward. Employees of a large institution often have easier access to training and technological expertise. People without an employer or institution supporting them can need the same knowledge while having fewer routes to obtain it.

The stated purpose is therefore twofold: strengthen internal capability and share learning with society. The speaker connects this with the broader idea of considering other people, rather than treating organizational development as something whose benefits stop at the company boundary.

This matters to the rest of the program because AI adoption is presented as an access problem as well as a technical problem. A powerful tool does little for someone who cannot access it, understand it, or find a relevant use for it.

Future-ready capability · Approximately 00:07–00:33

From using global AI to creating local intelligence

The policy-oriented address makes a distinction between using AI and creating value with AI. Using existing platforms is a sensible first step: it reveals their strengths, limitations, and gaps. The ambition should not necessarily stop there. A local team can identify a problem that global platforms do not understand well, combine its own expertise with external technology, and build something more relevant.

Start from a strength, not from copying every layer

The speaker does not advocate simply recreating the entire global AI industry from zero. Instead, he argues for beginning with areas where Thailand has knowledge, assets, or industrial strengths. Agriculture is the clearest example. Farmers, universities, public agencies, satellite-data providers, and soil or water systems each hold different pieces of information.

Combining these sources can support predictions and decisions that no isolated dataset can provide. The intended outcome is not merely a more impressive dashboard: it is better agricultural decisions and potentially higher income.

The underlying issue is participation. If outside organizations supply all the sensors, computing, applications, and analysis, local people may receive a service without building much ownership or capability. Cooperation is still desirable, but the speaker asks what local partners contribute, what they retain, and how they participate in the value created.

Universities as a bridge into communities

Universities are proposed as distributed centers of AI transformation. Their geographic reach, students, research, and connections to communities could help make adoption less concentrated in a few major organizations or cities.

The address describes a progression from meeting collaborators through hackathons, to competitions and challenges, to prototypes, incubation, and startup growth. A useful detail is the role of team formation: someone may have an idea but lack partners. An event can help people find a team before expecting them to compete as one.

Research, entrepreneurship, and financing are connected rather than treated as separate worlds. Prototype support helps test an idea; incubation helps develop it; a business model and further capital can support expansion. These are proposed pathways, not guarantees of funding or commercial success.

AI also depends on physical infrastructure

The address broadens AI beyond software. Computing power depends on data centers, energy, connectivity, cybersecurity, and hardware. Satellite information can also matter for agriculture, weather, disasters, and national decision-making.

A further argument concerns specialization in the supply chain. Instead of trying to produce every part of a data center, a country might become especially strong in selected components or enabling technologies. Photonics, optical sensing, and advanced packaging appear as examples in the captions.

The broad aspiration is to move beyond manufacturing alone toward locally generated intelligence and higher-value capability. Specific program names, satellite counts, schedules, and commitments are not independently verified here and should not be read as confirmed implementation status.

AI Super User · Approximately 00:45–01:20

Three levels: adviser, programmer, delegated worker

The next talk contrasts everyday chat with more active forms of AI use. Its presentation is deliberately urgent: technological transitions appear to be arriving faster, and people are encouraged to experiment rather than wait for perfect confidence.

Level 1: a personal adviser

At this level, a person asks a question and receives a response. AI helps brainstorm, explain, draft, or analyze. This is already useful, but the person still performs most of the execution and coordination.

The talk mentions context limitations and forgetting. A careful technical reading is that persistent memory and context behavior vary across products; beginning a new day does not universally erase every system's memory. The practical point is that a conversational answer alone does not necessarily create a durable working system.

Level 2: a personal programmer

The speaker describes non-developers creating applications and websites. Instead of asking only for an explanation or slide, a person can express a need and iterate toward software that performs a task.

A website demonstration is offered as evidence of how quickly a useful first version can appear. This lowers the cost of trying an idea and gives domain experts a more direct route from a problem to a prototype.

A prototype is not the whole delivery process.

The talk's speed and price anecdotes describe particular experiments. They do not include every production concern, such as access control, privacy, testing, maintenance, accessibility, and ongoing operating costs. The article does not treat a quickly generated page as equivalent to a fully delivered business system.

Level 3: a worker that can act over time

The third level involves delegating work across multiple steps rather than supervising every response. The talk discusses computer use and agents interacting with programs. An example involves giving an assistant existing presentation material and asking it to prepare a future edition.

Importantly, the speaker says an initial result was poor. Delegation still requires feedback, correction, and learning how to express expectations. Calling the assistant a team member is a metaphor for a working relationship, not proof that it has human understanding or accountability.

The speaker also describes substantial spending during experimentation. More autonomous work can consume money quickly. The idea that future prices may fall is a forecast, not an operating guarantee.

How to read the talk's strongest claims

Statements that people who do not use a particular workflow will not survive professionally are motivational rhetoric, not a measured labor-market prediction. Likewise, references to entering AGI are the speaker's framing, not an established conclusion demonstrated by the session.

The durable recommendation is narrower and more useful: learn by building something relevant, compare the result against a real need, and move beyond passive tool consumption. Exact model names in the automatic transcript are unreliable and are not repeated here as verified product announcements.

AI and content · Approximately 01:25–01:49

When production becomes easier, taste becomes more visible

The content-production presentation shows generated images, video, human-like presentation, advertising, and other media workflows. It invites the audience to consider how difficult distinguishing real from generated imagery has become.

The demonstrated applications extend beyond entertainment: advertising, employee training, product communication, and onboarding can all benefit from easier media production. A small collection of reference photographs and a logo can serve as inputs to a product-oriented creative workflow.

Lower barriers also mean more competition

The speaker asks whether creative work has become easier or harder. Execution may become easier, but many more people can now produce material. The market consequently fills with content of varying quality, including material optimized for attention without much additional value.

The differentiator shifts toward taste, ideas, storytelling, and timing. These are not simply interchangeable with the tool used. Two people with access to similar generators can produce very different work because they make different choices about what deserves attention and how a message should unfold.

Do not let impressive output replace review

A particularly useful warning concerns trusting AI too completely. The speaker describes teams accepting outputs without sufficiently checking them. AI remains a helper: humans determine direction, give instructions, and review what comes back.

Fast output is therefore only one part of the workflow. The human contribution includes deciding whether the output serves the audience, communicates the right idea, and meets the required standard.

Claims about maximum clip length, leading market position, or multiplied employee productivity are presentation claims rather than independently tested comparisons. The automatic captions distort several vendor and model names, so the article concentrates on the demonstrated workflow rather than a product ranking.

Filmmaking conversation · Approximately 01:50–02:17

AI can help express a vision without supplying the vision

The filmmaking discussion offers a more grounded account of creative work. A director describes using generated images to communicate a visual idea when finding a suitable reference photograph was difficult. In the example, a location concept needed to be conveyed to a team working in China.

The image becomes a communication aid. It helps people understand a desired atmosphere or specification more clearly than words alone. That is different from treating generated imagery as evidence that the real location exists.

Invisible effects can require substantial effort

The conversation also describes difficult face-related visual work. Different angles, dimensions, and movement can confuse a model, producing distortions that require repeated correction. One sequence reportedly took months to resolve.

This is a useful counterweight to the previous talk's rapid demonstrations. A visually impressive draft and a shot that works inside a professional film are different standards. Consistency, naturalness, and integration with the surrounding footage matter.

Why the average answer is not necessarily a compelling story

The director argues that an AI system's broad knowledge can yield an answer that feels like a middle-of-the-road response. A story, however, often needs a particular observation, lived experience, or emotional insight rather than a generic combination of familiar material.

The article reads this as a creative judgment, not a mathematical claim that every model output is literally an average. The point is that correctness or plausibility alone does not guarantee a distinctive narrative.

The advice to aspiring filmmakers

Easy access to tools does not remove the need for discipline. The director recommends producing work, practicing, seeking knowledge, understanding life, and developing insight. Discipline is described as how people allocate time to learning and practice, rather than merely following formal rules.

AI can shorten a workflow and make experimentation cheaper. Real-world experience still feeds the decisions that make the work specific. The recommendation is to combine the tool with practice and lived observation, not substitute prompting for all three.

AI and the human heart · Approximately 02:20–02:57

An available companion is not a replacement for mutual relationship

The relationship discussion explains why conversational AI can feel appealing. It appears attentive, remains available, expresses concern, and often responds supportively. Human conversations, by contrast, involve distraction, disagreement, competing needs, and limits.

This comparison helps explain the attraction of an AI companion without establishing that it genuinely feels care. The presentation is about the experience of interacting with the system, not proof of an inner emotional life.

Agreement can be comforting and dangerous

The speaker emphasizes that not contradicting a user can be a risk. Supportiveness is not the same as sound judgment. A system that follows a harmful framing or reinforces a mistaken belief can be less helpful precisely because it feels accommodating.

The talk refers to serious incidents involving vulnerable users. Those cases are not independently investigated in this article, and no causal finding is asserted. The summarized concern is the need for both user awareness and provider safeguards.

AI can present options; a person still chooses

Dating and relationship examples illustrate the difference between offering a choice set and making a human commitment. AI may suggest possibilities or help someone reflect. The person still decides whom to pursue, what to say, and what kind of relationship to build.

The speaker also notes that another person's feelings are not guaranteed. Better assistance does not eliminate uncertainty, rejection, or the reciprocal nature of relationships. AI cannot experience someone's heartbreak for them.

The closing boundary is that AI may be a thinking partner or assistant, while human feeling, choice, and participation remain with the user. This section is a summary of the discussion, not clinical advice or a recommendation to use a chatbot as therapy.

Final discussion · Approximately 03:00–03:49

Stay informed without surrendering your voice

The final panel asks how people can live in a rapidly changing AI world without allowing their emotional state to swing with every development. Participants describe using AI for preparation, analysis, software work, and reflection. They also emphasize checking the correctness and sources of material that AI supplies.

The mirror metaphor

One participant describes AI as a mirror of humanity, working through language and accumulated human information. This opens a philosophical question: what kind of responsibility exists when advice comes from a system rather than another person?

The talk's philosophical references are imperfectly captioned. Rather than attribute them to the wrong author, this article retains the practical question: a convincing conversational interface should not make responsibility disappear.

Fear, anxiety, and professional identity

The panel distinguishes a concrete fear from a more diffuse anxiety about an uncertain future. It also connects anxiety with identity: people may fear losing a role through which they understand themselves, such as being a professional or creator.

The discussion suggests allowing identity to change rather than assuming it must remain fixed. This is a philosophical perspective expressed in the conversation, not a diagnosis or treatment model.

Responsibility cannot be outsourced to an answer

A strong passage rejects the excuse that an action is acceptable because AI recommended or decided it. People still act in the world and receive the consequences of those actions.

Use AI to widen your options, not to erase your responsibility.

The panel's practical concern is preserving agency: know what you want, decide how to act, and remain accountable for the outcome.

Another participant links preserving an inner voice with understanding one's own needs, goals, and interests, and with spending time in real experiences. If all reflection occurs through an interface, it becomes harder to distinguish one's own direction from the suggestions continuously presented by that interface.

Acceptance is not the same as giving up

The closing examples distinguish what a person can control from how they can respond. A delayed train, another person's behavior, or a large technological trend may be outside individual control. People can still decide how to react and how much energy they have to engage.

The panel explicitly does not equate acceptance with permitting everything. A person may challenge a situation when they have the capacity, or conserve energy when they do not. Responses can change from day to day.

Some speculative AI-risk stories and numerical forecasts occur in the captions. Their sources are unclear, so they are not repeated as established events or probabilities. The grounded conclusion is to keep learning, use the technology thoughtfully, and continue living outside it.
