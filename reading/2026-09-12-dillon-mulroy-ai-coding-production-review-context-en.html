Dillon Mulroy: More Productive, Less Happy, Still Accountable

A Cloudflare engineer explains how he gets AI to produce code he can stand behind, why the work feels more exhausting, and why his workflow keeps design, context selection, and final responsibility in human hands.

Executive summary

He delegates the typing, not the engineering

Mulroy describes a sharp reversal. He was strongly skeptical of AI coding through much of 2025, then began using it extensively after a later generation of models changed what he could accomplish. By the time of the interview, he says AI had written most of his code for roughly six months. That sounds like an endorsement of hands-off automation, but his actual practice is almost the opposite: he steers the work closely, reviews every line, and keeps changes small enough to understand.

His story is neither simply optimistic nor simply anti-AI. He reports being more productive while enjoying his daily work less. He believes the tools can help produce high-quality software, but getting that quality requires considerable effort. He is skeptical of expensive autonomous loops and dislikes workflows that let agents decide which context matters without sufficient human control.

The core idea

AI is comparatively useful at implementing a well-defined function. The difficult part is deciding how functions, interfaces, errors, and dependencies should fit together. His workflow concentrates human attention on that composition problem before allowing the model to implement.

01 · A changed craft

The outcome is familiar; the route is not

The opening discussion distinguishes programming as a craft from software engineering as a business activity. Wortmann argues that the value of his work was never merely the code he typed. Tools have changed throughout his career, and he tries not to define his professional identity through a particular tool. Mulroy agrees at the level of responsibility: he is still applying experience, principles, and lessons from past mistakes to the systems he delivers.

What changed is the middle of the process. Instead of translating his understanding directly into code, he must get another system to express that understanding faithfully. This introduces a new skill: negotiating the shape of a solution with a model, controlling its context, and recognizing when its plausible implementation violates his design intent.

Mulroy references an essay by Ethan Niser about accepting a change in one's craft rather than resisting it indefinitely. The interview uses a transition from manual film editing to digital tools as an analogy. The point is not that every new tool is automatically good; it is that pride in craft can move from one production technique to mastery of another while preserving accountability for the result.

Not the same as blind acceptance

Mulroy explicitly says he is not letting unattended agents commit whatever they generate. His adoption of AI is paired with scrutiny, not a surrender of standards.

02 · The human cost

Why faster work can feel harder

His most distinctive observation is about the loss of small problem-solving rewards. In a traditional full-stack feature, an engineer first solves the large design problem: how the UI, endpoint, business logic, and storage should fit together. Then implementation creates a sequence of smaller challenges: choosing an API, handling an unexpected error, shaping a class, or fixing an awkward data transformation.

For Mulroy, those smaller challenges were satisfying. They provided frequent feedback and helped him enter a flow state. AI absorbs much of that implementation work, leaving him to move from one large design decision to the next. He describes this as more tiring, not less, even though the output increases.

Earlier rhythmMacro decision, then small wins

Architecture is followed by implementation problems, feedback, and a feeling of progress.

New rhythmMacro decision after macro decision

Delegated implementation removes some of the recovery and satisfaction between demanding choices.

Wortmann compares this with moving from individual contributor to engineering manager: the source of professional satisfaction changes when direct implementation is no longer the central activity. The analogy does not mean an AI user is literally managing a human team. It explains why the emotional adjustment can be substantial even when the business outcome improves.

Mulroy also describes feeling like a junior again. He has strong intuition about software design, but initially lacks equivalent intuition about making an agent produce the intended result. The frustration is familiar: knowing there is a good abstraction somewhere, but not yet being able to reliably reach it.

He sees signs that satisfaction may return as he becomes better at the new workflow. Nevertheless, the interview does not resolve the tension. Increased productivity, craftsmanship, and personal enjoyment are separate dimensions, and an improvement in one does not guarantee an improvement in the others.

03 · Production responsibility

A working demo is not a production standard

Both speakers insist that generated code needs critical assessment. A model can produce something that runs in minutes, but a production application must also be maintainable, safe, understandable, and consistent with the surrounding system. Liability and ownership do not disappear because the code came from a model.

Mulroy's practical rule is to read every line of generated code. He is still building the system holistically: checking whether the abstraction belongs, whether the error path makes sense, and whether the change creates a maintenance problem. The title's suggestion that he ships code he did not write must therefore be understood as a statement about authorship, not a statement that he ships code he has not inspected.

He also argues that the impact of an engineer's decisions can spread farther in less time when implementation accelerates. Consequently, macro-level design deserves greater care. Faster output can increase the consequences of a wrong boundary, a bad dependency, or a mistaken assumption.

Interpretation

The relevant safety property is not whether a human typed the implementation. It is whether someone can explain, validate, and take responsibility for what the implementation does. Reading the diff supports that responsibility, but does not replace execution, testing, security review, or operational evidence.

04 · Learning and careers

The junior pipeline is an open problem

The speakers are concerned that newer engineers can skip the struggles through which experienced engineers developed judgment. Mulroy says his architecture intuition comes from encountering many patterns across many domains and seeing which designs worked, failed, or turned out to be hype. Neither speaker has a complete answer for how the next generation acquires the same intuition when an agent can immediately supply a plausible solution.

They mention apprenticeship as a possibility, not an established solution. They also worry about a less desirable path: producing weak software faster and learning only when it breaks. The discussion should not be read as advice to create outages deliberately. It identifies a missing learning mechanism.

Wortmann argues that critical engineering judgment may be more valuable, not less, because someone must distinguish suitable code from unsuitable code. However, the interview does not provide labor-market research proving that all engineering roles will grow or that junior hiring is safe.

What a learning process would need to preserve

As an editorial implication, a useful junior workflow would require the learner to explain proposed boundaries, predict failure modes, inspect alternative implementations, and reason about test results. Otherwise, successful completion may hide a lack of understanding. The goal is to retain feedback and causal reasoning even if the learner no longer writes every line manually.

05 · Teams and role boundaries

More overlap does not mean every specialist disappears

Mulroy emphasizes product-minded engineering: understanding customers, experiencing their problems, and working effectively across organizational boundaries. AI makes it easier to explore another team's codebase or produce a prototype, so interpersonal skill and empathy become more important as engineers interact with more domains.

He gives a concrete example of an agent reacting to a pushed PR, generating Playwright tests, recording the interaction, and returning evidence for him to inspect. He describes a large time saving in his own workflow. This is an anecdote about a bounded task, not a measured claim that every QA activity can be reduced to seconds.

Wortmann pushes back on the idea that one generic product engineer can replace product management, design, QA, and other specialties. A prototype is useful because people can discuss something tangible, but it is not the same as a complete specification that supports engineering, design, testing, and marketing. Mulroy ultimately agrees that excellent product managers retain distinct value.

Their shared position is a change in handoffs. Product managers can communicate ideas with MVPs and prototypes rather than relying only on long documents. Engineers can evaluate those artifacts and bring them into production structure. That is not an endorsement of allowing an unreviewed prototype to go directly to production.

Layoff discussion: personal account, not a causal study

Mulroy discusses Cloudflare layoffs and attributes part of the reorganization to AI, while also acknowledging possible overhiring and other causes. Wortmann is more skeptical of AI-based layoff narratives. This article does not independently validate the headcount figures or conclude that AI alone caused the layoffs. Treat this portion as an employee's interpretation and a disagreement between the speakers.

06 · Autonomous loops and economics

Show the receipts, including the bill

The speakers question whether highly autonomous, multi-agent workflows demonstrated by people at AI labs are practical for ordinary developers and API-billed enterprises. Mulroy's position is conditional: he can imagine such workflows becoming useful, and can sometimes get promising experimental results, but he does not find the current combination of cost, quality, and consistency convincing for broad daily use.

His concern is not only a model's per-token price. A workflow can repeatedly consume large contexts, run several agents, perform exploration that never becomes useful work, or require expensive human correction. Quality also changes when the harness, tool definitions, or system prompt changes. A workflow that worked well on one release may become unreliable on another.

They discuss the possibility that organizations will allocate expensive agent work through explicit prioritization, rather than giving everyone an unlimited loop. That is a hypothesis about resource management, not a prediction established by data. They also speculate that capable open-weight models or improved token efficiency could change the economics.

A retention and routing caveat

A later exchange criticizes a particular model's enterprise data-retention arrangements and its ability to switch to another model during a session. The auto-captions render model names ambiguously, so this summary does not identify those names as verified products or repeat the retention claims as current policy.

Two general concerns are still understandable: a business needs to know which retention terms apply to its endpoint, and a routing change may affect behavior and cache economics. Retention, training use, and prompt caching are distinct issues; the interview's compressed discussion should not be treated as a definitive vendor-policy comparison.

07 · The working environment

A small harness and a fast review loop

Mulroy describes a terminal-oriented setup with Ghostty, a terminal multiplexer transcribed as Herdr, the Pi coding harness, and Plannotator. The specific terminal choices organize projects and running processes, but the deeper preference is stability: he wants the harness to do relatively little and to change infrequently.

His reason is behavioral control. If tools or system instructions change constantly, it becomes harder to know why the model is responding differently. A minimal harness provides a more understandable baseline. He likes Pi's small surface and customizability without relying on heavy customization.

This preference is consistent with Pi's primary documentation, which describes a minimal terminal harness and an extensible core rather than built-in subagents or plan mode. That does not make Pi universally superior; it makes its defaults compatible with his chosen workflow.

Review locally before creating team review work

Plannotator supplies a browser-based surface for annotating plans and code changes and feeding feedback back into the agent session. Mulroy uses it to inspect local diffs before asking teammates to review a PR. He can go file by file, leave comments, and send corrections back through the same loop. The project's code-review documentation confirms this local diff and annotation workflow.

He also uses annotation of Markdown files and the agent's last message to refine a plan. The important idea is not a magic command. It is reducing the friction between noticing a design problem, expressing a precise correction, and seeing the agent revise its work. Plannotator's command overview describes both document annotation and review functions.

08 · Change size

Keep the unit of understanding manageable

His delivery style has not fundamentally changed: small, atomic, incrementally stacked PRs. He gives roughly 300 to 800 lines as the size he tries to keep an individual piece of work around. That is his personal working range, not a universal quality threshold. A 50-line security change may be harder to review than hundreds of generated fixtures.

Stacked PRs let subsequent work build on an earlier branch before it merges. A second PR targets the first branch, forming a sequence of dependent changes. This can preserve small review units while allowing a larger feature to progress. The interview mentions several tools and upcoming platform support, but the summary does not rely on those time-sensitive availability claims.

The principle is reviewability. If an agent produces a giant diff, the author may no longer be able to understand it deeply before sharing it. Smaller increments reduce the chance that increased output simply transfers a larger inspection burden to teammates.

09 · Specification

His technical spec looks like code, not a PRD

Mulroy does not start by attempting to write a flawless prose document containing every detail. Instead, he iterates with the agent until they share a sufficiently precise understanding. He then captures that understanding in a technical specification dominated by TypeScript types and explicit execution structure.

The specification identifies interfaces, boundaries, adapters, and implementations. He asks the agent to outline the call stack it will implement: whether it is changing an existing path or creating a new one, what each step receives, what it returns, and which errors can occur. Finally, he requires an outline of the tests that will guide implementation, including a red-green-refactor approach.

ShapeTypes and interfaces

What values exist and which component owns them?

CompositionBoundaries and adapters

Where does one responsibility end and another begin?

ExecutionCall paths and errors

How does the request travel, and how can it fail?

EvidenceTests before implementation

Which observable behavior proves the design works?

He describes these specs as potentially hundreds of lines long, but their density comes from structural detail rather than extensive narrative. The purpose is to constrain composition. Once the shape of a function and its place in the system are agreed, he is more comfortable letting the agent fill in the implementation.

Wortmann finds this attractive because he is often too exploratory to author a complete PRD upfront. He has also found tests established before implementation more useful than asking a model to invent tests after the code already exists. These are reported workflow experiences, not controlled comparisons proving a specific multiplier.

Why this is different from merely asking for more detail

A long prompt can still leave architectural choices unresolved. Typed boundaries, explicit error paths, and expected tests make disagreements visible before implementation hides them behind working code.

10 · Context engineering

Explore broadly, bring back only what matters

Before implementing a feature, Mulroy often asks questions to which he already knows the answers. He might ask the agent about Hono, a framework API, or a storage pattern used in the codebase. This is not because he needs a tutorial. He is checking and building the agent's understanding while using his own knowledge to judge the result.

He uses Pi's conversation tree to isolate these explorations. After exploring one topic, he returns to an earlier point in the conversation. He can carry a summary back automatically, guide the summary, or manually preserve the final useful message through copying or a Markdown file. He then explores another topic without keeping all of the previous branch's research chatter in the active context.

Main conversation: define a new endpoint
  • Branch A: establish framework behavior and relevant APIs.
  • Branch B: establish storage behavior and existing codebase patterns.
  • Branch C: establish testing requirements and constraints.
Return to the main path with selected conclusions

Combine concise, inspected summaries into the technical spec. Leave unnecessary exploratory history outside the active path.

This is a deliberate separation between exploring and committing context. A branch can contain a large amount of discussion, but its useful contribution to implementation may be a small set of constraints. The human decides what crosses that boundary.

Conversation navigation is not a Git rollback

Mulroy explicitly distinguishes returning to an earlier conversation point from undoing repository changes. Files and Git history do not automatically rewind with conversational context. An agent resumed from an earlier point must still reason about the current filesystem.

He can keep a long session with multiple branches for feature implementation, review, and end-to-end testing. The session is a navigable work history, not necessarily one ever-growing active context. Labels and saved summaries help him refer to useful parts without bringing every intermediate exchange into the next task.

11 · Delegation limits

He wants to own the context selection

Mulroy describes his tree workflow as doing some of the work of subagents himself. His objection is not that parallel exploration can never help. It is that the main agent often decides what a subagent should investigate and which summary returns, and those summaries may omit the information he actually wants in the main context.

He has developed a practical intuition for which context improves the model's output. Since that intuition is not yet something he trusts an agent to reproduce reliably, he prefers to control branch exploration and summary selection directly. He acknowledges that this intuition remains approximate and the system is nondeterministic.

Wortmann sees some value in subagents for a large, coherent chunk of work that would otherwise overwhelm the active context, but is skeptical of ornamental role-based agent teams. Their criticism is a position about their workflows, not proof that every multi-agent system is ineffective.

Implication for an agent platform

As editorial synthesis, useful delegation should have an explicit task boundary and an explicit return contract. The supervisor should know what was checked, what evidence was collected, what remains uncertain, and what conclusions must be preserved. Naming a worker “architect” or “reviewer” does not supply those guarantees.

12 · What he may automate next

Remove repetition without removing judgment

Near the end, Mulroy imagines a queue-based system that prepares parts of his current workflow: exploring technologies, drafting the typed spec in the format he prefers, and presenting work for iteration. That is a proposed future direction, not the unattended system he says he already runs.

He is also trying to capture recurring design and review preferences in skills so an automated first pass can catch familiar problems. His success criterion is modest: if it eliminates a few repetitive corrections or saves five minutes of review, it helps. He does not expect it to be perfect or even particularly good at the beginning.

This is an important closing contrast with the earlier skepticism about autonomous loops. He is not opposed to more automation. He wants automation to earn its place by reliably reducing a known burden while leaving important decisions inspectable and correctable.

Editorial assessment

The strongest lesson is about control, not a tool stack

The interview offers a coherent explanation for why an experienced engineer can benefit greatly from AI while remaining dissatisfied with it. Much of the productivity comes from delegating well-bounded implementation; much of the remaining effort comes from design, context curation, and review. Those activities become the bottleneck.

The practical recommendations are persuasive where they make that bottleneck visible: inspect local changes before sharing them, use small increments, agree on typed interfaces, specify failure paths, and curate research summaries. The story is less conclusive when it generalizes from one engineer or one company to all developers, careers, or agent economics.

It is also worth keeping quality separate from stylistic resemblance. Code that looks like something the author would write can still contain a bug. Alignment with an engineer's taste is valuable, but correctness needs independent evidence. Likewise, human review is essential to this workflow but does not make every release safe by itself.

Bottom line

The transferable idea is a human-owned engineering loop: establish understanding, make the design concrete, delegate a bounded change, and inspect the result. More autonomy should follow demonstrated reliability and useful cost savings, not precede them.

Takeaways

What to remember

  1. Delegating code generation is not delegating responsibility.
  2. Productivity and enjoyment can move in opposite directions.
  3. AI may remove small satisfying tasks while concentrating harder decisions.
  4. A working result is not automatically a maintainable production result.
  5. Experienced judgment comes from feedback; junior workflows need to preserve that learning.
  6. Prototypes can improve cross-functional communication without replacing specialist ownership.
  7. Autonomous workflows must justify both correction effort and token cost.
  8. A stable, minimal harness can make behavior easier to understand.
  9. Review locally before transferring work to teammates.
  10. Small changes preserve the author's ability to inspect the system deeply.
  11. Specify types, boundaries, call paths, errors, and tests before implementation.
  12. Exploration history and implementation context are different things.
  13. Moving through a conversation tree does not undo file changes.
  14. Delegation needs a useful return contract, not just a role name.
  15. Automate repetitive corrections first, and expand only when the results earn trust.
