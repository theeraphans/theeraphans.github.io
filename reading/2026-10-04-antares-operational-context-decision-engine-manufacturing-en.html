The hard part is capturing how the business decides

Devin Kearns explains why CustomAI Studio is building Antares: a system that models operational context, combines it with business rules, and routes the next decision. The long-term ambition is larger than another AI tool. Antares is meant to become the coordination layer for a distributed, AI-native manufacturer.

Executive summary

The product is not the model. It is the operating context around the decision

Kearns starts with a contrarian business thesis. Most applied AI companies sell tools to an existing operator: AI for a law firm, manufacturer, accountant, or hospital. He believes the larger opportunity is to use AI to run the operating company itself. Antares is the bridge between those positions. It begins as software that helps a manufacturer understand and route decisions, but the intended destination is an operating layer that coordinates production across factories, suppliers, warehouses, people, agents, and machines.

The key observation is that building an AI workflow is often easier than learning why a business behaves the way it does. The formal process is rarely the whole process. Staff remember which supplier becomes unreliable in a particular season, which spreadsheet is trusted over the ERP, which policy is routinely overridden, and which customer requires an unusual approval path. This tacit context determines whether an apparently reasonable automated action is correct.

Antares is presented as three connected systems. A knowledge graph and ontology describe the business and its dependencies. A governance model captures rules, policies, permissions, and exceptions. A decision engine combines current events with that context and governance to choose or recommend the next action. Each decision and outcome then enriches the operating model.

  • The LLM is only one decision layer. Deterministic rules should handle stable logic; models handle bounded judgment; people retain high-risk and ambiguous decisions.
  • Operational context includes reasons, exceptions, dependencies, trusted sources, informal practices, and human preferences, not only records and process diagrams.
  • A knowledge graph is necessary but not sufficient. The system also needs temporal validity, provenance, confidence, policy, feedback, and measurable outcomes.
  • Manufacturing is attractive because delays and waste often emerge between organizations, where information is fragmented and decisions are slow.
  • The long-term value is decision ownership. If the software allocates work and coordinates the network, the boundary between software vendor and operator becomes thinner.
  • The final vision, a manufacturer with no single factory, is a thesis. The video does not present audited production results that prove the network-scale model.

Evidence boundary. This article separates the architecture described in the video from external support for the general problem. Claims about more than 60 customers, more than 100 production systems, a $50 million waste case, and a possible 20 percent recovery are statements by the creator. No case study, customer identity, baseline, or measurement method is supplied in the video, so those figures should not be treated as independently verified results.

Business thesis

Move from selling the tool to operating the outcome

Kearns divides a company into people, processes, and systems that produce an outcome. AI changes all three. It can assist or replace parts of human work, alter the process itself, and connect systems that previously required manual coordination. Selling a tool improves one part of this structure. Owning the decision layer can reshape the structure.

This is why the phrase "vertical AI is dead" needs a careful reading. The proposal is still deeply vertical. Antares must understand manufacturing entities, constraints, economics, quality rules, suppliers, and production sequencing. What Kearns rejects is stopping at vertical SaaS that merely exposes an AI feature. His preferred company uses domain-specific intelligence to take responsibility for an operating result.

Three levels of value capture

LEVEL 01

AI tool

Helps a person complete a task. The customer still coordinates the process and owns every operating decision.

LEVEL 02

Decision layer

Observes events, applies context and policy, and chooses or recommends what should happen next.

LEVEL 03

AI-native operator

Accepts demand and coordinates people, suppliers, factories, logistics, and machines to deliver the outcome.

The transition between these levels is not automatic. A tool can recommend an action without bearing the cost of a wrong one. An operator must manage contracts, safety, quality, cash, liability, and recovery. The business model becomes more valuable only because the responsibility becomes more demanding.

Decision architecture

The LLM is only one decision layer

Kearns argues that many production decisions should not be delegated to a general model. A stable rule is cheaper, faster, easier to audit, and more predictable as code. A model becomes useful when the input is messy and the judgment can be constrained. A person should remain responsible when stakes are high, evidence is incomplete, or an exception has no reliable precedent.

The decision stack described in the video

01Deterministic

Hard-code repeatable rules, calculations, validation, and policy checks. Use software when the decision boundary is known.

02Model judgment

Use an LLM or specialist model for bounded interpretation, classification, extraction, prioritization, or recommendations under explicit constraints.

03Human decision

Escalate material exceptions, uncertain evidence, legal or safety exposure, and decisions where accountability cannot be delegated.

The important design move is routing. The system must decide which layer owns the case before it decides the case itself. That routing policy needs thresholds, uncertainty signals, approval boundaries, and a record of why a path was selected. Without those controls, "human in the loop" becomes a vague promise rather than an enforceable operating rule.

Operational model

Operational context is the missing model

ERP systems contain transactions. BI systems aggregate metrics. Process documents describe the intended workflow. None of them necessarily explains why an experienced employee made a particular choice. Kearns calls this missing layer operational context: the reasons, exceptions, dependencies, and preferences that connect an event to the action the company actually takes.

What the operating model must capture

Entities and relationships

Customers, orders, materials, machines, suppliers, warehouses, people, and the dependencies between them.

Decision rationale

Why one supplier, route, schedule, approval, or exception was chosen over another.

Rules and exceptions

Formal policy, local workarounds, override conditions, authority limits, and regulatory constraints.

Source trust

Which record is authoritative when the ERP, spreadsheet, email, and operator memory disagree.

Time and state

When a fact became true, how long it remains valid, and which operating state existed when a decision was made.

Outcome evidence

What happened after the action, whether the KPI moved, and whether a person corrected the recommendation.

The examples in the video make the problem concrete. One mediator may be informally avoided. One attorney may have scheduling preferences that never reached the official system. A supplier may be trusted in one season but not another. An invoice process may depend on a spreadsheet that the organization never declared as a system of record. An AI system that sees only the documented procedure will repeatedly rediscover these exceptions through failure.

External manufacturing research supports the general gap. NIST describes the need to contextualize data across design, planning, manufacturing, and inspection, and separately notes that smart-manufacturing systems often lack explicit integration of human knowledge and human-machine interactions. That supports the problem statement. It does not validate Antares as the solution.

Antares architecture

Context plus governance becomes a decision engine

The video describes Antares as a knowledge graph and ontology, but the graph is not the end product. It supplies the structured context required by a governance model. The decision engine then evaluates a new event, identifies the affected entities and dependencies, applies policy, and selects a next action or escalation.

The Antares operating loop reconstructed from the transcript

01

Ingest

Read structured records, documents, messages, conversations, and operational events.

02

Model

Map entities, processes, dependencies, exceptions, trusted sources, and current state.

03

Govern

Apply policy, permissions, constraints, thresholds, and human approval requirements.

04

Decide

Choose a deterministic action, bounded model judgment, recommendation, or escalation.

05

Learn

Record execution, outcome, correction, and new context so the operating model can improve.

For an incoming email, the useful question is not simply "What does this message say?" It is "Given the sender, order, current capacity, policy, dependencies, history, and permissions, what should happen next?" That is a decision problem with language as one input, not a chat problem.

Platform implication. Separate the business contract from the model call. Represent the event, decision options, evidence, constraints, allowed actions, confidence, and escalation path as typed data. A model may help interpret evidence, but policy and authorization should remain independently enforceable.

Model limits

A knowledge graph is necessary but not sufficient

A graph can connect entities and expose dependencies, but a production decision system needs more than topology. It must know whether a fact is current, where it came from, how reliable it is, who may override it, and what happened after the system acted. Otherwise the graph can preserve stale folklore with the same confidence as a verified policy.

  • Provenance: every consequential fact should point to its source, collector, and extraction method.
  • Temporal validity: preferences, capacity, supplier quality, and policy change. The model must represent when a statement was valid.
  • Confidence and disagreement: conflicting records should remain visible until resolved, not collapse silently into one answer.
  • Authorization: knowing the recommended action does not grant authority to execute it.
  • Outcome measurement: a decision is not improved merely because it was recorded. The system needs a defined success metric and a counterfactual or baseline where possible.
  • Recovery: production decisions need idempotency, audit logs, rollback, and a person who can stop the system.

Capturing context also does not prove causality. A system may observe that delayed orders correlate with a supplier, a shift, or a transport route without proving which intervention will improve the result. Root-cause recommendations should be tested against historical data, operational constraints, and controlled changes before the system acts at scale.

Why manufacturing

The waste often sits between organizations

Manufacturing creates a dense coordination problem. Brands, research teams, contract manufacturers, raw-material suppliers, warehouses, maintenance crews, and logistics providers all hold fragments of the operating state. A delay may begin as an inventory mismatch, capacity conflict, quality issue, or approval bottleneck, then surface later as missed production or excess material.

Kearns argues that much of the waste is not caused by a lack of data. It comes from information reaching the decision too late or without the context needed to act. Antares would map the value chain, combine records with conversations and human judgment, identify where a KPI is being lost, and recommend an intervention.

This is plausible, but a manufacturing decision engine must respect the physical system. Lead times, batch sizes, machine capabilities, maintenance windows, quality controls, labor rules, supplier contracts, transportation constraints, and safety requirements cannot be inferred away. A good architecture combines learned interpretation with deterministic planning, constraint solving, and human authority.

Expansion path

From one factory to a production network

The proposed progression starts narrowly. Antares becomes the decision engine for one manufacturer. As more decisions and outcomes pass through the system, its operating model becomes richer. It can then coordinate a larger production environment and eventually route work across a network of factories.

At network scale, the allocation problem includes capacity, materials, supplier reliability, transport cost, delivery time, quality, and the capabilities of each site. Demand enters the network, and Antares decides where and how the physical world should fulfill it. Kearns describes an organization that may not own one central factory. It coordinates a changing set of factories, humans, agents, robots, suppliers, and logistics partners.

The proposed growth path

The no-factory manufacturer is a thesis, not a result. The video explains the strategic direction but does not show a live network, allocation benchmark, audited savings, safety case, or customer deployment at this scale. Read it as the destination Antares is being designed toward.

Practical blueprint

How to build toward this idea without pretending the model knows the business

  1. Choose one decision, not one department. Define the trigger, options, authority, evidence, success measure, and cost of a wrong action.
  2. Map the current decision path. Interview the people who do the work, observe exceptions, and compare the documented flow with the actual one.
  3. Create a source register. Name the authoritative system for each fact and preserve conflicts, timestamps, and provenance.
  4. Separate context from policy. Context describes the operating state. Policy constrains what may happen. Do not bury permission rules inside a prompt.
  5. Use the smallest decision layer that works. Prefer code for stable rules, a model for bounded interpretation, and a person for high-risk ambiguity.
  6. Run in recommendation mode first. Compare recommendations with human decisions and outcomes before granting execution rights.
  7. Measure calibration and business effect. Track abstention, escalation, override, error severity, cycle time, waste, and downstream outcomes, not only model accuracy.
  8. Expand only after recovery works. Add autonomous action after audit, rollback, access control, incident response, and ownership are tested.

This sequence keeps the architecture honest. It makes the organization prove that it understands the decision before asking a model to automate it.

My take

The strongest idea is organizational memory with enforceable policy

The strongest part of the Antares thesis is not the knowledge graph or the agent layer. It is the recognition that reliable automation depends on the reasons behind the work. Most organizations have pieces of those reasons scattered across people, messages, spreadsheets, policy documents, and exceptions. Turning that material into an inspectable operating model would make both people and software better at decisions.

The difficult part is governance of the model itself. A living model can become a powerful shared memory, or it can become a confident archive of stale assumptions. The difference is provenance, versioning, ownership, validation, and feedback. Antares will be credible to the extent that it makes uncertainty and disagreement visible rather than hiding them behind a clean recommendation.

The business thesis is also sharper than "AI will automate manufacturing." Kearns is arguing that the most valuable software will own allocation and coordination decisions. That can create real leverage, but it also moves the company closer to operational accountability. The strategic upside and the operational burden arrive together.
