VOC Inquiry Intelligence

Turn customer emails and calls into reviewed inquiry data that teams can act on.

Customer messages were data, but not yet usable data

Customer-service teams handled more than 3,000 emails and 600 calls a day. The useful information was inside individual messages: the type of inquiry, the customer's concern, and the cases that needed attention. Reading those messages was necessary operational work, but it did not automatically create consistent data for reporting.

At MiSUMi, I worked on an inquiry-intelligence workflow that turns emails and calls into structured information, with people reviewing the result before it reaches downstream business systems.

Prepare, classify, then review

The workflow first prepares the message. Emails supply text directly; recorded calls need transcription. Analysis then assigns an inquiry type and extracts information for customer-feedback analysis. The taxonomy covers 108 inquiry types, so classification needs more context than a short list of broad labels.

Retrieval supplies relevant inquiry definitions to the analysis workflow. LlamaIndex and Milvus support that context, while Azure OpenAI supports transcription and classification. LangChain and CrewAI coordinate the workflow, and LangFuse provides traces for investigation.

The important handoff is the review portal. People can inspect the classified inquiry and urgent cases before the reviewed data is delivered to CRM and Power BI. The original message remains the evidence behind the classification.

Do not confuse retrieval with a correct label

A retrieved definition helps an agent interpret a message; it does not prove that the selected label is correct. The system needs both a consistent taxonomy and a way to inspect uncertain or problematic results. Traces help identify where the workflow went wrong, while review keeps operational judgment in the process.

My contribution covered AI analysis, retrieval, and workflow observability. The work connects message-level processing to the data that operations teams use, rather than stopping at a generated summary.

One message can contain several business questions

Consider an illustrative email: “My shipment is late, and I need to change the delivery address.” It contains a shipment concern and a requested action. The example is fabricated for explanation; it is neither a customer record nor an assertion about the production label policy.

The taxonomy needs to say what an inquiry type represents. A topic, a requested action, and a responsible department are different dimensions. Combining those meanings can cause disagreement even when the model and reviewer both understand the message.

Retrieval supplies relevant definitions, while classification selects an output that fits the business taxonomy. Preserving the retrieved context for investigation helps distinguish a wrong definition from a wrong interpretation.

Calls add another failure point. A misheard product identifier or negation can change the prepared text before classification begins. A reviewer needs access to the evidence behind the label, subject to the organization's data-access policy.

Make the downstream record predictable

A summary is helpful to readers, while reporting needs consistent fields and known meanings for missing values. Otherwise identical issues can fragment into different dashboard categories because the generated wording changes.

A taxonomy change can alter reporting even when customer behavior stays constant. Merging two labels should therefore be visible in both the evaluation set and the interpretation of historical trends.

Inspect mistakes hidden by an overall score

With 108 inquiry types, an aggregate score can hide a weak category. Microsoft's classification documentation explains class-level precision, recall, F1, and confusion matrices. Precision asks how often a predicted label is correct; recall asks how often an actual category is found. Those concepts help evaluate this workflow, though the documentation describes a custom classifier rather than this LLM pipeline. Microsoft classification evaluation concepts.

A recommended evaluation would sample by channel and inquiry type, with human-reviewed expected labels. Email and transcript cases should be inspectable separately. Related messages from one thread should stay together when splitting cases, so near-duplicates do not make the held-out set artificially easy.

Evaluation also needs an agreed unit. If a message contains several intents, a single-label score and a multi-label score answer different questions. The annotation policy should settle that before anyone compares model versions.

The confusion patterns matter operationally. Mistaking two routine topics may require a small correction. Missing an urgent issue may have a different business cost. A useful rubric names those costs and inspects the affected cases instead of treating every mistake as interchangeable.

Measure the work left for reviewers

Correctness is one part of the workflow. A system can improve its classifications while sending too many routine messages to reviewers. Removing review from difficult cases can save time while raising the cost of mistakes. Both properties need acceptance criteria.

A proposed operational report would track review volume, corrections, unresolved cases, processing failures, and time spent waiting at each handoff. Those are proposed measures rather than published results. They would help identify whether the bottleneck lies in transcription, analysis, review, or delivery.

Duplicate ingestion deserves its own test. The same email can arrive through a retry or appear again in a thread. A repeat should not silently inflate inquiry counts. A sample-based replay test can check how the integration treats repeated source messages without exposing customer content publicly.

Traceability also has a privacy cost. Raw messages can contain personal or commercial information. Any public example should be anonymized and approved for disclosure, and internal investigation access should follow the organization's policy.

A shared view of customer inquiries

The result is a repeatable path from incoming messages to reviewed inquiry data. That supports customer operations and reporting across a high-volume workload. Production accuracy, response-time measurements, and internal deployment details are not included in this public account.
