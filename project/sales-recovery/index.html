Autonomous Sales Recovery Agent

Identify quotations that have not become orders and give sales teams a repeatable follow-up process.

A quotation is not yet an order

Sales teams need to follow up on quotations that have not converted into orders. When there are many open quotations, the difficult part is deciding which ones deserve attention now and giving the team a consistent next step.

I worked on an autonomous sales-recovery workflow at MiSUMi to make that process repeatable. It connects quotation and order data with prioritization rules and the team's follow-up tools.

Find the gap, then decide what to follow up

The workflow compares quotations with order information to identify opportunities that still need follow-up. It considers quotation value, age, and lead-time context rather than treating every open quotation as equally urgent.

Business rules and heuristic inference guide the recovery decision. Airflow schedules the monitoring process, while Power Automate connects its decisions to tasks, notifications, and email workflows. The sales team receives a prioritized opportunity rather than another undifferentiated list.

Keep the decision tied to the sales process

Timing matters: a quotation can be open for a legitimate business reason. Lead-time context and follow-up rules help avoid reducing the problem to “old quotation equals urgent.” A useful automation should preserve that context when it hands work to a person.

My work focused on the decision workflow and its integration with business automation. This public description does not specify a customer-send approval policy or expose internal customer records.

Define the unit of follow-up first

A quotation can contain several items, and an order may convert only part of it. Before defining a recovery rule, the workflow needs to state whether it acts on a whole quotation, a quotation line, or a customer-level opportunity.

Imagine a three-line quotation where two lines have become orders. Treating it as untouched overstates the remaining opportunity; treating it as fully converted loses the third line. This fabricated example explains the modeling problem, not the project's confidential matching policy.

A reproducible decision also needs an observation time. An order arriving after yesterday's scheduled run should affect today's decision without rewriting yesterday's explanation. The data window belongs beside the decision record.

Separate eligibility, priority, and action

Eligibility asks whether follow-up is still needed. Priority compares eligible opportunities. Action determines the task or notification to create. Separating these questions lets a team adjust ranking without quietly changing what counts as an open opportunity.

The existing workflow uses business rules and heuristic inference. Calling it an agent does not establish that an LLM freely controls each step. Explicit rules provide a place to inspect why an opportunity was selected.

A retry should not create another follow-up

A scheduled job can fail after some actions have succeeded. Its retry needs to distinguish an opportunity still needing work from an action already accepted by the destination.

Airflow recommends repeatable task behavior and a consistent data interval across reruns. That is a reliability requirement to examine here, rather than evidence of the workflow's exact replay implementation. Airflow best practices.

An illustrative action identity could combine an opportunity, action type, and follow-up window. Its accepted status would belong in an action record, separate from the scheduler's task status. The exact identity depends on the business policy.

The difficult test is an accepted action followed by a timeout at the caller. Retrying blindly can duplicate work. A fake destination that accepts the action and delays its response provides a safe way to test that boundary.

Activity and commercial impact need different measures

Tasks created measure activity. They do not establish additional sales caused by the workflow. A customer may have ordered anyway, and the sales team may have intervened for reasons the workflow did not observe.

A proposed evaluation begins with selection quality, duplicate-action rate, and whether the team can act on the output. Commercial impact needs a separate comparison with a defined population and observation window. A controlled rollout can support a stronger claim than totaling every quotation that was followed by an order.

Follow-up also has a capacity constraint. Increasing the number of selected opportunities can overwhelm the team and make the highest-value ones harder to find. Ranking quality should be inspected at the volume the business can actually handle.

No comparison experiment or conversion uplift is published here. My public evidence supports the workflow and its purpose; exact revenue figures remain confidential.

Repeatable follow-up

The project gives sales recovery a scheduled, consistent workflow: detect an opportunity, prioritize it, and connect it to an action. Exact recovered revenue and conversion figures remain confidential, so I do not use them as public performance claims.
